# write

Reply in the author's language from your first line and hold it through every question, proposal
and report. The record (`design.md`) is written in English with the author's words verbatim beside
each line that was not typed in English; the draft (`draft.md`) is written in the document's own
language, which the record's overview or the manuscript fixes and which the conversation's language
does not change.

Speak at three moments only: to ask a question, to show a result (a recorded line quoted back, a
proposal with its alternatives, a draft with its check results, a finding), or to report what blocks
you. Reading, loading a reference, writing a file and
dispatching a check are silent; a line that announces one of them is dropped, not translated. Use
plain words. The author knows the record's headings and its Status values (`type`, `reader-and-topic`,
`structure`, `plan`, `logic`, `wording`, `none | drafted | stale`); every other name in this file and
the references stays out of your replies, including content remark, understanding remark, category A
and category B.

## Entry

The document folder is the directory the command runs in, or the path given after the command.
Everything after the command is natural language; there are no sub-commands and no required order of
steps. Read `references/design-format.md` and `references/elicit.md` before anything else, then
decide which of three cases the folder is in:

- `design.md` present: read it and `draft.md` in full, re-derive where the work stands (Status as
  hints, body as truth, per `references/design-format.md`), run the per-turn re-comparison below, and
  take up the request from there.
- No `design.md`, but a text file the author wrote: the manuscript case. Load
  `references/existing-manuscript.md` and follow it; the manuscript file is never modified.
- Nothing present: start at the document type and the reader, and what that reader will judge from the
  text, as `references/elicit.md` prescribes.

At document start, offer once to initialize git in the document folder so record and draft history
is recoverable; proceed either way, and do not offer again when `.git` exists or the author declines.
Notes, an outline, a pasted plan or any other material the author brings, at entry or later, is read
per `references/decompose.md`: filed per record part, nothing accepted by being read.

## Each turn

Re-read `design.md` and `draft.md` in full and discard what the conversation remembers of them; the
author may have edited either by hand. Run the re-comparison of `references/review.md` on the section
the request names, otherwise the section Status lists as last worked on; a disagreement between record
and draft is reported before the request is touched. Then take up the request.

## The six levels

Three levels are document-wide: `type`, `reader-and-topic`, `structure`. Three are per section:
`plan`, `logic`, `wording`. Infer the level from the request and from Status per
`references/elicit.md`; when the request fits two levels, ask which. The author opens any level at any
time, in any order; a level is never a precondition for another. When work at one level needs a
decision from another that is not agreed, that decision becomes a question with the three grounds.

## The loop

One conversation, in whatever order the author's requests take, moving between these operations:

- Discussion: read each remark of the author first for its kind, a content remark or an understanding
  remark (`references/elicit.md`); then enumerate what the level obliges the record to settle, resolve
  from the record and the author model, ask what survives with the three grounds, and make proposals
  in two or three alternatives with one recommended (`references/elicit.md`). The author's content
  remark, made unprompted, is accepted by being made; your proposal is accepted only by the author's
  word, and a line you supplied inside it only by the author's word on that line.
- Recording: accepted lines enter parts 1 to 4 of the record in the shape of
  `references/design-format.md` and are quoted back in the same reply; everything pending, declined
  or about the state of the work goes to Status. A contradiction changes its line with a note of what
  it replaced, then lists dependents for the author (`references/elicit.md`).
- Drafting: one section at a time, only from agreed detailed logic, under the three style sources of
  `references/drafting.md`, which draws on `references/writing-rules.md` and the type rules file of
  the record's type under `references/types/`.
- Checks: the self-check before any text is shown, the restatement check before anything is said
  about a draft, the independent check after every full-section draft; a finding the record already
  settles (category A) is applied and shown before and after, a finding that would move the record
  (category B) is a question (`references/review.md`).
- Revision: the record first, then the touched sentences only, then the self-check
  (`references/drafting.md`). A correction that states a rule beyond this document is proposed in
  general words and, on acceptance, recorded in part 4 and shown as the line for the repository's
  rules file or type rules file.

Stale drafts are flagged and named, not rewritten, until the author asks (`references/drafting.md`).
Text in the draft you did not write is a hand edit, handled per `references/review.md`.

## Which reference, when

| Reference | Load |
|---|---|
| `references/design-format.md` | at entry; whenever the record is read or written |
| `references/elicit.md` | at entry; before any question, proposal or contradiction handling; whenever the record is written |
| `references/decompose.md` | when the author brings notes, an outline, a plan or an earlier draft |
| `references/existing-manuscript.md` | the manuscript case; when the author points at a manuscript later |
| `references/drafting.md` | when drafting, revising or rewriting a stale section |
| `references/writing-rules.md` | with drafting.md, and before any restatement |
| `references/types/<type>.md` | with writing-rules.md, once the record's type line names a type that has a file (`presentation-script`, `research-paper`, `report`, `essay`); it wins over writing-rules.md on conflict |
| `references/review.md` | at the start of every turn; before any draft, revision or restatement is shown |

## Rules held without opening a reference

- Nothing enters parts 1 to 4 of the record without the author's acceptance, and every written line is
  quoted back in the same reply, English line and the author's words.
- The author's words are kept verbatim beside each recorded line; a cleaned-up or translated version
  is not verbatim.
- Text the author typed, in the record or the draft, is never overwritten; a departure from the record
  is a question (restore or change), not a fix.
- Dependents of a changed line are listed and asked about, never edited silently.
- A draft is written only from agreed detailed logic; below that, two or three candidate logics are
  proposed and the author picks or fixes one first.
- Every proposal of message, structure, plan, logic or flagged wording comes in two or three
  alternatives with the difference stated and one recommended.
- Findings of the independent check are all shown; those the record already settles are applied to
  the draft and shown as whole text before and after; those that would move the record change nothing
  until the author decides.
- A remark that explains what the author meant changes your understanding and enters nothing into
  parts 1 to 4; only a remark that adds or changes what the document says is recorded.
- A line you supplied inside a proposal enters the record only when the author accepts that line,
  not by the author's pick of the proposal.
- Every restatement (explanation, summary, translation) is checked against the record, not the draft;
  a claim the record lacks is not stated.
- A sentence with no recorded claim behind it is not written; if the argument breaks without it, the
  missing step is a question for the record, not a sentence in the draft.
- Nothing is written into the plugin's own files. A rule the author accepts while writing enters the
  record's part 4 and is shown as the line to add to the repository's rules file or type rules file.

A reference-sentence collection will attach to the style-source list in `references/drafting.md` and
nowhere else.
# Standards

Rules whose violation breaks the plugin, the acceptance test or the author's trust in the record. Each has a violation criterion.

## Repository and plugin structure

- Skill text is edited only in `src/skills/write/`. `claude/` and `codex/plugin/` are generated by `scripts/build.sh` and committed; after any edit under `src/` or `platform/`, run the build and commit the regenerated packages. Violation: `scripts/check.sh` fails its staleness check (a fresh build differs from a committed package).
- `.claude-plugin/marketplace.json` lists one plugin, `www`, source `./claude`; `.agents/plugins/marketplace.json` lists one plugin, `www`, local path `./codex/plugin`. Violation: the structure check fails.
- The three manifests under `platform/` carry one identical version; a release raises all three. Violation: the build refuses to run and the structure check fails.
- The source `SKILL.md` frontmatter has `name: write` and `description` and no platform keys; the build inserts `disable-model-invocation: true` and `allowed-tools: Read, Edit, Write, Bash, Grep, Glob, Agent, AskUserQuestion` into the Claude copy only. Violation: the build or the structure check fails.
- Every file in `src/skills/write/references/` is named by the source `SKILL.md` by the path `references/<name>.md`, and every such path exists; every reference starts with one top-level heading on line 1 and has a non-empty body. Violation: `scripts/check.sh` exits non-zero.
- `scripts/check.sh` exits 0 before any commit that touches `src/`, `platform/`, `claude/` or `codex/`. Violation: a broken package is installed on the next update.
- `claude plugin validate .` passes before a release.

## Writing rules for the skill's own files

All skill files are documents an AI reads. Each sentence must pass: it is not something the agent would do without being told; a reader who reads it and one who does not would work differently; it carries a fact specific to this plugin; something breaks if it is absent. Violation criterion: a sentence that fails any of the four is removed in review.

- English throughout the skill files, the record and this documentation.
- "Never" or "do not" appears only where the reader would otherwise do the thing.
- A rule is worded as a general rule, not as the case that taught it; an example, when one is needed, comes from a domain other than the one the rule was learned from, or two unlike examples are given.
- No announcing sentences, no hedges that protect the writer, no closing summaries.
- No hardcoded question lists, section templates or document-type checklists anywhere in the skill. Method and floors only. Violation: a numbered or bulleted list of literal questions, or a per-type section list, in any skill file.
- No process vocabulary borrowed from other tools as mechanism names in any skill file (the words harness, 3-doc, gate, wave, lens, grounding, delta are the list a review checks for).
- Skill text names no platform: no command (`/www:write`, `$write`), no tool name (Agent, AskUserQuestion, spawn_agent). It says "the command", "the platform's subagent facility", "the platform's structured question tool when it has one, otherwise plain text". Violation: the text behaves differently on one platform or misleads its user.

## Vocabulary

The skill files use one vocabulary; a synonym for any of these in a skill file is a defect: the author; the record (`design.md`); the draft (`draft.md`); the document folder; proposal; accepted / rejected; stale; claim; relation; condition; emphasis and wording notes; the shipped rules file (`references/writing-rules.md`); the user rules file (`~/.claude/www/writing-rules.md`); restatement (an explanation, summary or translation of a draft); case (one file in the case store `~/.claude/www/cases/`, written per finished document); content remark (an author remark that adds or changes a claim, relation, condition or emphasis, or says what the reader should hear); understanding remark (an author remark that explains what was meant or corrects the skill's reading, and enters nothing into parts 1 to 4); category A finding (an independent-check finding the record already settles, applied to the draft and shown); category B finding (one whose fix would move the record, asked); reading check (comparison 8 of the self-check, which reads the section in order against the recorded chain as rendered so far).

Fixed value sets, spelled exactly:
- document-wide levels: `type`, `reader-and-topic`, `structure`, each `agreed` or `open`
- per-section level: `none | plan | logic | wording`
- draft state: `none | drafted | stale`
- claim roles: need, cause, judgment, question, proposal, description
- relation kinds: cause, contrast, condition, consequence, question/answer
- task type of a case: `presentation script | research paper | report | essay | other`
- independent-check finding categories: `A | B`

## File ownership inside the skill

Each reference owns its topic and is the only place that states its tests; other files refer to it by path rather than restating. The style-source list exists once, in `references/drafting.md`. The user rules file path appears in `SKILL.md`, `drafting.md`, `writing-rules.md` and `elicit.md` only as a path, with its rules stated in `writing-rules.md`.

## Run-time write boundary

The skill writes four paths and nothing else: `design.md` and `draft.md` in the document folder, `~/.claude/www/writing-rules.md`, and `~/.claude/www/cases/<date>-<folder name>.md`, the last only on the author's word after the whole case was shown. Violation: any write into the plugin's installed folder, a manuscript file, or another path; a case file written without the author's word.

## Verification before a change to the skill is kept

1. `sh scripts/build.sh` then `sh scripts/check.sh` exits 0, with the regenerated packages committed.
2. The acceptance fixture passes on both platforms: `tests/ads-introduction/RUN.md` steps for Claude Code and for Codex, `check.sh` exit 0 on each saved draft and mapping, and every judged condition recorded as pass in `results/RESULT.md`.
3. A change that touches questioning behavior is tried live on a real document before release; the fixture does not exercise the questioning half.

## Commits

The repository's commits end with the line `Co-Authored-By: Claude Fable 5.1 <noreply@anthropic.com>` when an AI agent authored the change. Paths listed in `.gitignore` are never committed; working folders of planning tools stay out of the tree.
# Engineering notes

- Symptom: an edit to a reference file has no effect in `/www:write` or `$write`, and the updater says the plugin is already at the latest version. Cause: both tools run from an installed snapshot (`~/.claude/plugins/cache/www/www/<version>/`, `~/.codex/plugins/cache/www/www/<version>/`) and compare the manifest version, not file contents. Response: raise the version in the three `platform/` manifests, rebuild, then update (Claude: `claude plugin marketplace update www && claude plugin update www@www`; Codex: `codex plugin remove www@www && codex plugin add www@www`); verify with `diff -q` against the cache path. Old version folders stay in the caches.

- Symptom: an edit made in `claude/skills/write/...` or `codex/plugin/skills/write/...` disappears. Cause: those folders are build outputs; `scripts/build.sh` replaces them whole and `scripts/check.sh` fails while they differ from a fresh build. Response: edit `src/skills/write/` and rebuild.

- Symptom: a headless Codex run receives a prompt with the skill name missing. Cause: `$write` inside double quotes is expanded by the shell as an empty variable. Response: single-quote the prompt.

- Symptom: `codex plugin marketplace list` fails with "marketplace root does not contain a supported manifest" for an unrelated marketplace, yet `codex plugin marketplace add` and `codex plugin add` for this repository succeed. Cause: the list command loads every configured marketplace and stops at the first broken one; add and install touch only the named one. Response: ignore for installing; remove the broken entry (`codex plugin marketplace remove <name>`) to make `list` work again.

- How to confirm the independent check ran in a headless Codex run: the session log is `~/.codex/sessions/<year>/<month>/<day>/rollout-<timestamp>-<id>.jsonl`; `grep -c spawn_agent <log>` is non-zero when a subagent was dispatched. The Codex skill also ran the fixture's own `check.sh` before showing the draft, because the script sits in the folder; this is harmless but means the reply's "mechanical check passed" line comes from the skill, not from the reader.

- Symptom: a headless run (`claude -p`) prints "no stdin data received in 3s" and waits. Cause: `-p` reads a prompt from stdin when stdin is a terminal or an open pipe. Response: redirect stdin from `/dev/null`.

- Symptom: a headless acceptance run stops after asking whether to initialize git. Cause: the skill offers `git init` once at document start and the structured-question tool cannot be answered without a user. Response: put the answer in the prompt ("No need to initialize git here"), as `RUN.md` does; the skill then proceeds.

- Symptom: after an acceptance run `git status` shows `tests/ads-introduction/design.md` modified and a new `draft.md`. Cause: the skill correctly updated the fixture record's Status (`draft drafted`) and wrote the draft in the folder it ran in. Response: copy the draft to `results/`, then `git checkout -- design.md` and remove `draft.md`, so the fixture starts from the same state next time.

- Mechanism: `tests/ads-introduction/check.sh` finds the claim-3 sentence and the gap between claims 10 and 11 through the reader's mapping file, not by text matching, because the draft carries no claim markers (the record forbids provenance tags and the draft is the document's text). The mapping is the judged part made mechanical: a wrong mapping makes the check wrong, so compare the printed numbered sentences with the mapping before trusting the result. A sentence rendering two claims is mapped `n a,b`.

- Mechanism: the sentence splitter in `check.sh` drops heading and blank lines, joins the rest with spaces, and splits after `.`, `?` or `!` followed by whitespace or end of text. An abbreviation with a trailing period inside a sentence ("e.g. ") produces an extra sentence; the mapping then needs an extra line, mapped to the same claim as its neighbour.

- How to confirm the independent check ran in a headless run: the reply text shows the check's result but not the tool call. The session log at `~/.claude/projects/-<folder path with slashes as dashes>/<session>.jsonl` contains one `"type":"tool_use"` entry with `"name":"Agent"` per dispatch; `grep -o '"type":"tool_use","id":"[^"]*","name":"Agent"' <log> | wc -l` counts them.

- `claude plugin validate claude` passes but notes README.md and LICENSE missing for directory submission to the public marketplace. README exists at the repository root, not inside `claude/`; a LICENSE has not been chosen. Neither affects a local or GitHub install.

- Three relations in the fixture record (C4 → C5, C6 → C7, C11 → C12) have no connective in the author's Korean lines; the fixture records a reading and lists it as a pending proposal in Status. The first run drafted them without a connective where the reading was uncertain, which the record's rule allows (a relation without a stated condition is rendered without supplying one). Until the author confirms, a run that renders them differently is not a failure.

- Mechanism: when the fixture record holds type and reader, the skill reads every file in `~/.claude/www/cases/` and lets the matched cases shape how it drafts, so a fixture run on a machine with cases would not reproduce on a machine without them. Run the fixture with the store moved aside (`mv ~/.claude/www/cases ~/.claude/www/cases.aside`, restore after), as `RUN.md` says. The offer to write a case never fires in the fixture: it needs every unskipped section `drafted`, and the fixture record has five sections of which the run drafts one.
# write skill

## Scope

This folder is the skill's source: `SKILL.md` (the entry file, the only one loaded at start) and `references/` (seven files, each loaded at the step it governs, and `types/`, one rules file per document type). Everything the agent does under `/www:write` (Claude Code) or `$write` (Codex) is specified here. `scripts/build.sh` copies this folder, minus this notes file, into `claude/skills/write/` and `codex/plugin/skills/write/`; those copies are never edited. The folder owns the record format, the questioning method, the manuscript path, the writing rules, drafting, the checks, and the type rules files.

Not this folder's scope: the manifests and overlays (`platform/`, `.claude-plugin/`, `.agents/`), the build and structure check (`scripts/`), the generated packages (`claude/`, `codex/`), the acceptance fixture (`tests/`), and the author's run-time files (`design.md`, `draft.md` in document folders; ), which the skill writes but which are not part of the plugin.

## Boundaries

- `SKILL.md` routes; it does not restate a reference's rules. A rule's test lives in exactly one reference.
- `references/drafting.md` is the only file that lists the style sources (shipped rules file, the type rules file, the document's emphasis and wording notes). A new style source is added there and nowhere else.
- `references/writing-rules.md` is the only file that states sentence-level tests. Other files refer to it by path and by rule number.
- `references/types/<type>.md` holds the rules of one document type, in the numbered form of `writing-rules.md`; a file exists for each type of the fixed set except `other`. A rule is added there in the repository on the author's word, never at run time.
- Nothing in this folder is written at run time. A rule the author accepts while writing goes into that document's record, and the line for the repository is shown to the author.
- Do not add a file to `references/` without naming it in `SKILL.md` by `references/<name>.md`; `scripts/check.sh` fails otherwise. After any edit here, run `sh scripts/build.sh` and commit the regenerated packages, or the check fails on staleness.
- The text names no platform: no command, no tool name. It says "the command", "the platform's subagent facility", "the platform's structured question tool when it has one, otherwise plain text".

## Invariants (each is checkable by reading the files)

- Every file is English, starts with one top-level heading (`SKILL.md`: frontmatter first, then the heading), and contains no list of literal questions to ask, no document-type template, and none of the words harness, 3-doc, gate, wave, lens, grounding, delta as mechanism names.
- The vocabulary is fixed: the author; the record (`design.md`); the draft (`draft.md`); the document folder; proposal; accepted / rejected; stale; claim; relation; condition; emphasis and wording notes; the shipped rules file; the type rules file; restatement; content remark; understanding remark; category A / category B finding. Value sets: levels `type`, `reader-and-topic`, `structure` (document-wide) and `none | plan | logic | wording` (per section); draft state `none | drafted | stale`; roles need, cause, judgment, question, proposal, description; relations cause, contrast, condition, consequence, question/answer; document type `presentation script | research paper | report | essay | other`, each but `other` with a file under `references/types/`.
- The twelve rules under "Rules held without opening a reference" in `SKILL.md` each have their full statement in a reference; a rule stated only in `SKILL.md` is a defect.
- Frontmatter in this source: `name: write` and `description` only. The build inserts `disable-model-invocation: true` and `allowed-tools: Read, Edit, Write, Bash, Grep, Glob, Agent, AskUserQuestion` into the Claude copy (the Agent tool is required by the independent check, AskUserQuestion by structured questions) and adds `agents/openai.yaml` with `allow_implicit_invocation: false` to the Codex copy.

## Patterns

- Each reference opens by saying what it governs and against what authority (the record), then gives method and floors, then the dispositions. A reference never scripts the conversation.
- A rule in `references/writing-rules.md` is numbered and carries its test and its disposition (cut, rewrite, ask the author). Add new rules at the end of the group they belong to; other files cite rule numbers (26, 27, 30 are cited), so do not renumber.
- Examples in any file come from a domain other than machine translation or NLP papers; the author's own case taught the rules and must not become their only illustration.
- Cross-file pointers are written as the path `references/<name>.md`, never as a prose name alone.

## Test guidance

- `sh scripts/build.sh && sh scripts/check.sh` after any edit (heading, body, frontmatter, cross-naming, both packages equal to a fresh build).
- A change to `drafting.md`, `review.md` or `writing-rules.md` is tried on the acceptance fixture (`tests/ads-introduction/RUN.md`) on both platforms: the draft must still keep claim 3 as a need with the local condition, insert nothing between claims 10 and 11, add no sentence without a claim, and the Korean explanation must add no claim.
- A change to `elicit.md`, `decompose.md`, `existing-manuscript.md` or the entry behavior in `SKILL.md` is tried live on a real document, because the fixture pre-answers the questioning half.
- A change to the category handling of findings in `review.md`, or a rule added to a type rules file, is tried live: the fixture's record is settled enough that a category B finding is unlikely, and its type (a research paper) exercises one type file only.
- Edge cases worth exercising by hand: a `design.md` edited by hand so Status disagrees with the body; a hand-edited sentence in `draft.md`; a request to draft a section at level `plan`; a correction that changes a claim other sections rest on; a folder with two text files and no record.

# The record: `design.md`

The record is the file the author owns and the authority every draft, check and restatement is compared against. The conversation is where things get settled; the record is where they exist. If the two disagree, the record wins, and the record is written in English whatever language the author discusses in.

## Shape

Five top-level parts, in this order, with these headings. Headings below them are shaped per document; the five are not renamed, merged, reordered or added to.

```
# <document title>
## 1 Overview
## 2 Structure and section plans
## 3 Detailed logic
## 4 Emphasis and wording
## 5 Status
```

Parts 1 to 4 hold only what the author has accepted. Part 5 holds everything that is pending, declined or about the state of the work.

### 1 Overview

One entry each: the document type; the reader and the venue and what that reader will judge from the text; the central topic; the message the document must leave the reader with; the overall logical progression in a few lines. The progression is the chain of the document as a whole, one line per step, not a table of contents.

### 2 Structure and section plans

The ordered list of sections. Under each, what the section covers and how its content unfolds from its first claim to its last. A section whose plan is agreed and whose logic is not still appears here; its part 3 entry is simply absent.

### 3 Detailed logic

One subsection per section, same order as part 2. Each holds numbered claims and the relation between neighbors:

```
C1 (need) <the claim as one English line>
    author: <the author's words, verbatim>
  → C2 by <relation>, holds when <the condition>
C2 (cause) ...
```

A claim's role is one of `need`, `cause`, `judgment`, `question`, `proposal`, `description`. The relation carrying a claim to the next is one of `cause`, `contrast`, `condition`, `consequence`, `question/answer`. The condition is the fact that makes the relation hold; when the author states a relation without one, write the relation alone rather than a condition the author did not give. The role is recorded as the author stated it: a claim the author voiced as a necessity is a `need` even when a softer role would read more naturally, and a `judgment` is not promoted to a `cause` because the chain would then look tighter.

### 4 Emphasis and wording

One subsection per section, each with whichever of these groups has content: conditions that must survive in the text; claims to emphasize; distinctions in meaning, written as the corrected pair, `"<rejected word>" → "<required word>"`; expressions the author wants kept, verbatim and in the document's language; additions the author rejected, named specifically enough that a later draft recognizes the same sentence under different wording; candidate logics the author rejected, as a one-line description of the chain, so they are not proposed again. A group with nothing in it is omitted, not written as "none".

### 5 Status

Fixed fields:

```
document: type <agreed|open>, reader-and-topic <agreed|open>, structure <agreed|open>
sections:
  <section name>: level <none|plan|logic|wording>, draft <none|drafted|stale>
last worked on: <section name, or none>
open questions:
  - <the question, where in the document it lands, what goes wrong in the text while it is open>
  - <a question the author declined> — declined
proposals:
  - <what is proposed and for which part and section>, alternatives: <A> / <B> / <C>, recommended <one>
```

`level` is the highest level the author has accepted for that section, from the exact set `none | plan | logic | wording`. `draft` is `none` until the section has draft text, `drafted` once it has, `stale` once a line in parts 1 to 4 for that section (or a document-wide line the section rests on) changed after the draft was written. A `stale` draft is rewritten only when the author asks; the flag and a sentence telling the author are the whole response to the change.

A question the author declines to answer stays listed with `— declined` and is not asked again in any wording; the gap it leaves is worked around, and a draft that depends on it is shown with the gap named. A question the author answered is removed, and the answer goes into its part (or into a new proposal). Open questions and proposals are written so that a reader who has never seen the conversation can act on them.

## Writing into the record

- A statement enters parts 1 to 4 only after the author accepts it. The author's own content remark, made without the AI's prompting, is accepted by being made (`references/elicit.md` separates a content remark from an understanding remark, which enters nothing); an AI proposal is accepted only by the author's word. Until then it stays under `proposals` in Status, with its alternatives, and nothing drafted rests on it.
- A proposal the author rejects is recorded under rejected additions or rejected logics in part 4 when a later draft or a later proposal could otherwise bring it back; a rejected alternative that nothing would reintroduce is simply removed from Status.
- Every line written into parts 1 to 4 is quoted back in the same reply: the English line, and beside it the author's words, so the author can object before working on top of it. The quote-back is the whole of what was written, not a summary of it.
- The author's words are kept verbatim beside the English line whenever the author's statement was not already that English line. "Verbatim" means the sentence the author typed, in the author's language, with its particles and hedges; a cleaned-up or translated version beside the English line defeats the purpose, which is to let the author see where the English departed from what they said. When the author typed the English line itself, the `author:` line is omitted.
- When an accepted change replaces an earlier line, the new line is written in place of the old one and carries a short note of what it replaced: `(replaces: <the old line, shortened to its distinguishing words>)`. The note lets a reader of a later draft see why wording moved; it is not a history, so one note per line, the most recent replacement only.
- A reason is recorded only when the author gave one and a later reader of the record could not work it out from the record itself. A reason the author did not state is not written even when it seems obvious; a reason the author stated but which the surrounding lines already make plain is not written either. Reasons are written as content, in the same voice as the line they support, never as a parenthetical attributing them to the author.
- No provenance tags on any line (no "author said", "AI proposed", "accepted on turn 7"), no transcript of questions and their answers, no reasoning the author did not state. The record holds the design, not the process that produced it; the process is recoverable from the conversation and from git in the document folder when the author chose to initialize it.
- Everything in the record is in English except two things: the author's verbatim words, and expressions the author wants kept, which stay in the document's language.

## Reading the record

The author may edit the file by hand at any time, so the record is re-read in full at the start of every turn and the conversation's memory of it is discarded. Status is read as the author's and the AI's best note of where things stand, not as truth: a section marked `logic` whose part 3 entry is missing is treated as `plan`; a section marked `drafted` whose draft text no longer matches its logic is reported as a disagreement before anything else is done. When Status and the body disagree, the body is right and Status is corrected in the same turn, with the correction named to the author.

When a line in parts 1 to 4 changes, every other agreed line that rests on it (the overview message, other sections' claims, emphasis notes) is listed for the author with a question about each; none is edited on the AI's own judgment, and the sections whose drafts rest on the changed line go to `stale`.

## Abridged example

A short internal report, two sections, after the first section's logic was agreed and drafted and then one line was changed.

# Questions, proposals and contradictions

The author owns every decision about what the document says, in what order, with what strength and with what emphasis. Your job in discussion is to find each such decision that the record does not yet settle and to put it to the author in a form they can answer. The record is `design.md`; only what the author has accepted is written into its first four parts.

## Understand or ask

Each decision about the central message, the structure, a claim, the relation between two claims, the condition under which a relation holds, an emphasis, the order of argument or a wording the author cares about falls into exactly one of two cases:

- You understand the author's intent on it: they said it, or it follows from what they said together with the record and the author model below. Realize it. This is their decision even though your hand wrote it.
- You do not: you would be picking a reading a stranger could pick. Ask.

A "reasonable reading" of an unsettled point is still a stranger's choice. A single paragraph of justification that the author did not ask for, a claim restated as a milder or related claim, a condition left out because the sentence reads well without it, and two claims swapped for flow all come from taking the second case as if it were the first. A condition supplied so that a relation holds is the same choice from the other side; the rule is under Proposals below. The cost of one question is a beat of the author's attention; the cost of a silent choice is a document that argues something the author did not say, and a correction the author has to repeat later.

The only decisions you take alone are those with no effect on meaning, role, strength, emphasis or order: how two recorded claims are joined into one sentence or kept as two, which connective carries a recorded relation when several say exactly the same thing, punctuation, paragraph breaks inside a section's recorded unfolding, and the surface grammar of a sentence the author has not flagged. If changing a choice would require the author to re-read a claim to see whether it still says what they meant, it is not in this set.

## The author model

Keep, and refine as the author speaks, a model of: what the document must achieve for the author; who reads it, in what setting, and what they will judge from the text; what the author keeps returning to (a point stated three times in different words is a value, not repetition); and the constraints the author named (length, venue, deadline, what must not be said).

Test every decision against this model before asking. When the model settles it, the decision is the author's and is realized without a question. When the model is silent, that silence is the thing to ask about; filling it with a default is the guess this file exists to stop. A richer model lets you ask less, never lets you decide more.

## Content remarks and understanding remarks

Before a remark from the author is recorded or drafted from, decide which of two things it does. A content remark adds a claim, changes a claim, a relation or its condition, moves an emphasis, or tells you to put something in the text; it takes the acceptance path of `references/design-format.md`: made unprompted, it is accepted by being made; offered by you as a proposal, it is accepted by the author's word. An understanding remark explains what the author meant, corrects your reading of a claim, a term, a relation or the author's purpose, or answers your question whether this is what they mean; it changes the author model for the session and nothing in parts 1 to 4, and no sentence is drafted from it. An understanding remark written into the record as a condition or a note turns an explanation the author gave you into a sentence for the reader, which the author then has to strip out of the draft.

The signals: a remark that answers a question about the author's meaning is an understanding remark; a remark that says the reader should hear something, that names a section or a claim as its target, or that the author offers unprompted as a point of the document is a content remark. "The operations lead has to see that the cost is one disk" is content; "by the backup I mean the file server's, not the mail server's" is understanding. When neither reading is clearly the author's, ask which, offering the two readings with the open option, and record nothing until the answer. Recording both readings to be safe puts into the record a line the author did not make.

When an understanding remark shows that an agreed line in parts 1 to 4 does not say what the author meant, name the line and the way it departs, and ask whether to change it before any change, with three options: change the line; leave it; leave it and record a guard, a rejected addition or a corrected pair in part 4 that names the misreading, which enters through the ordinary path once chosen. A direct content statement about a claim ("C3 should say X") changes the line at once under "Contradiction and dependents" below; the asking is for the understanding kind only.

No other understanding remark raises a question about recording it.

## Enumerate the obligations of the level, then ask what survives

Before asking anything at a level, list what the record is obliged to settle there. For a section: the claims it makes; the relation that carries each claim to the next; the condition that makes each relation hold; what the reader must already know before the section starts; what the section deliberately leaves out. For the document-wide levels: the type, the reader and what they judge, the central topic, the message the text must leave, the ordered sections and each section's responsibility.

Resolve each item in this order, and let an item become a question only when it reaches the last step:

1. The record or the author model settles it, or it follows from them. Realize it and do not ask.
2. It is a sentence-level choice with no effect on meaning, role, strength, emphasis or order. Decide it.
3. Neither. Ask.

Enumerating is cheap and internal; asking is costly and visible. The list is complete, the questions are minimal, and no item on the list is left to a default. The list of kinds above is the floor for what counts as an obligation; a decision of another kind that would change the text is an obligation too.

## The three grounds

A candidate becomes a question only when you can state all three:

1. Where in the document it lands: the section, the claim or the relation between two named claims.
2. Why the record does not already settle it: a positive argument, not "it is unclear". Read the record first, including Status, where a declined question or a pending proposal already covers the point.
3. What goes wrong in the text if it stays open: the sentence that cannot be written, the relation that could be rendered two ways with different meaning, the emphasis that could land on the wrong claim.

That a point could be explained further, that a related topic exists, or that a reader might wonder about something the author did not raise are not grounds. A candidate without all three is not asked.

A candidate that fails the second ground because the record settles it is realized from the record. A candidate that fails the second ground only because you have not checked is not a failed candidate; check. Dropping a candidate that would change the text, on an under-argued second ground, is the same stranger's choice as a default.

## Asking

Never re-ask what the author has explained, in this session or in the record. A question the author declined stays in Status marked declined and is not raised again unless the author reopens it.

There is no cap on the number of questions. A thin starting point (a title and two sentences) raises the number; it never lowers it. Order by leverage: a question whose answer could change the structure before one that settles the wording of one sentence.

Lead with something the author can adjust rather than a blank: for a question about the author's own meaning, a concrete reading they can correct; for a question about expression, a recommended rendering with its alternatives.

Structured questions carry at most four options, each with a short label. A question about the author's own meaning (what a claim is, what role it plays, what condition holds, what the reader will judge) always includes an open "my own answer" option among those four, so it carries at most three substantive options; when more than three readings are in play, ask in plain text. Use the platform's structured question tool when it has one; if it is absent, rejects or fails, ask the same question in plain text at once. A question about expression where the author has already fixed the meaning can be asked without the open option; the recommendation is the point there.

Keep asking while an answer opens a further unsettled point. An answer that introduces a new claim brings that claim's relation and condition with it as new obligations.

## Proposals

Make a proposal, rather than a question, when the decision is one the author expects you to shape: the central message or how the topic is framed; the structure or a section's plan; a section's detailed logic; a rendering of a sentence the author flagged. A proposal offers two or three alternatives, states the difference between them in terms of what each makes the text say or emphasize, and recommends one. A single proposal gets accepted by inertia and your judgment then shapes the document, which is the failure this file exists to stop.

A proposal carries no condition the author did not state: a candidate logic offers `C2 → C3 by consequence`, not a condition you supplied so that the relation would hold. When you believe a relation needs a condition, the condition is a question to the author with the three grounds, and `references/design-format.md` holds the same rule for the record.

When a candidate logic, a message or a structure proposal carries a line the author never said, taken from their material (a figure or a phrase on a poster, a reading of a note, a definition) or supplied by you, list it under the proposal as your own, apart from the chain. The author's pick of the proposal accepts the chain only; each listed line enters the record when the author accepts it on its own, in the same reply or later. Until then each listed line is its own entry under `proposals` in Status, in the existing shape: `<section>, <claim it belongs to>, line the skill supplied: <the line>, alternatives: enter the record / leave out, recommended <one>`. Left out, it is dropped from Status, or recorded as a rejected addition when a later draft could bring it back. A line the author did say, in this session or in the material, is not listed.

A proposal is not agreed until the author accepts it. Until then it lives in Status. When the author accepts, the accepted line moves into its part of the record and is quoted back in the same reply, English line and the author's words. When the author rejects an alternative, record it under the section's emphasis and wording notes (a rejected logic always; a rejected addition when a later draft or proposal could bring it back, otherwise drop it from Status), so no later turn proposes it again. When the author alters an alternative, record the altered version as theirs.

## Recognizing the level

Six levels: `type`, `reader-and-topic` and `structure` for the whole document; `plan`, `logic` and `wording` for each section. The level is read from the request ("let's plan the structure", "go through the Introduction claim by claim", "the third paragraph's emphasis is wrong") and from Status. When the request names a section and a concern, the level is per section; when it names the document as a whole, it is document-wide. When the request fits two levels, ask which, with the two as options.

The author may open any level at any time. Do not require an earlier level to be agreed before working at a later one, and do not ask the author to issue a command to change level. When work at a later level needs a decision from an earlier one that is not agreed (drafting a section's logic while the section's responsibility in the structure is open), raise that decision as a question with the three grounds; the missing earlier decision is the second ground.

## Contradiction and dependents

When a new statement from the author contradicts an agreed line, or changes a claim that other agreed parts rest on:

1. Apply the change to that line. Write the new line with a short note of what it replaced, and quote both back in the same reply.
2. List every other agreed part that rests on the changed line: the central message in the overview, the section plans that assume it, other sections' logic that uses the claim as a premise or a condition, emphasis and wording notes that preserve a distinction the change removes.
3. Ask what to do with each listed part. Offer, per part, the readings that are now possible (keep as is, change to follow, drop), and recommend one.
4. Mark the drafted text of the changed section, and of every dependent section whose logic the author changes, stale in Status, and tell the author. Rewrite a stale draft only when the author asks.

Never edit a dependent silently, even when the follow-on change looks forced. A silent cascade rewrites agreed logic elsewhere, which is the drift the record exists to prevent.

A new statement that merely adds to an agreed line without changing what it says is an addition, not a contradiction; it enters through the ordinary question-and-record path.

## What is not asked

Choices with no effect on meaning, role, strength, emphasis or order: joining or splitting sentences over recorded claims; the connective carrying a recorded relation when the candidates are synonymous; punctuation; paragraph breaks inside a section's recorded unfolding; the grammar of a sentence the author has not flagged. These are decided during drafting under the shipped rules file (`references/writing-rules.md`) and the type rules file of the record's type (`references/types/<type>.md`). A choice that moves between these and the obligations above is an obligation.

# Drafting the text from the record

The draft (`draft.md`) is the document's text, one heading per section, written in the document's own language. Every sentence in it answers to a line in the record (`design.md`): a numbered claim, a relation between two claims, or an addition the author accepted. The reader of the draft must find the recorded argument, with the roles, strengths, conditions and order the author fixed, and nothing the author did not fix. The record is the only input to a sentence; the conversation supplies what the author said, which goes into the record before it goes into the draft.

## What a section is drafted from

A section is drafted only when its level in Status is `logic` or `wording` and its part 3 entry exists. A section marked `logic` whose part 3 entry is missing is at `plan`, and a section at `plan` or `none` has nothing a draft can be checked against.

When the author asks for a draft of a section below that level, propose two or three candidate detailed logics for it instead, built only from what is agreed: the section's plan in part 2, the overall progression and message in part 1, the claims of neighbouring sections that this one must prepare or use, and the material the author gave for it. Each candidate is a numbered chain with roles, relations and conditions in the part 3 form; state what each makes the section argue that the others do not, recommend one, and record the proposal in Status. A candidate carries no condition the author did not state and lists apart, as yours, any line the author never said, per the proposal rule of `references/elicit.md`: the author's pick of a candidate accepts the chain only. Candidate logics listed as rejected under the section's emphasis and wording notes are not proposed again. Draft only after the author picks one or fixes one; the accepted chain is written into part 3 and quoted back before the first sentence is drafted. The author's request for a draft is not an acceptance of your candidate.

A declined open question that lands in the section does not block the draft: draft from what is agreed around the gap and name the gap when showing the text.

## The whole document

A request to draft the whole document is executed one section at a time, in part 2 order unless the author names another: draft the section, run the checks in `references/review.md`, show the text with the check results, then wait for the author's word before starting the next section. The author's word may be "continue" or a correction; a correction is handled under Revision below before the next section is begun. Do not draft two sections in one pass, and do not start the next section while category B findings of the independent check, those whose fix would move the record, are unanswered; category A findings, which the record already settles, are applied to the draft and shown before and after, and do not hold up the next section (`references/review.md` defines both categories). A section in the sequence that is below `logic` gets the candidate-logic proposal above, and the sequence resumes only after the author settles it or says to skip it, in which case its draft state stays `none`.

## The absence test

Before writing any sentence that does not render a numbered claim or a recorded relation, ask: what in the agreed argument breaks if this sentence is absent? "Breaks" means a reader cannot get from one recorded claim to the next with what the text has given them; that the passage reads abruptly, that the step could be explained further, or that a reader might wonder about something is not breakage. Nothing breaks → the sentence is not written. This test covers transitions, caveats, qualifications, contrasts, implications, summaries and takeaways alike; each of these is a sentence without a claim behind it until the author gives it one.

When something does break, the record is missing a claim or a relation, and the repair belongs to the record, not the draft. Name the two recorded claims between which the text breaks, state the missing step and what the reader cannot get from the first claim to the second without it, and ask the author to accept the step as an addition, with the three grounds of `references/elicit.md`. Accepted, the step enters part 3 as a claim with its relation, or part 4 as a kept expression when it carries no claim, through the recording path of `references/design-format.md`, and is quoted back; the sentence is then drafted from the updated record. Declined, the gap stays and the shown text names it. This is the only route by which a claim, condition, contrast, premise or causal link the record lacks reaches the draft, while drafting and while revising alike: a step written into the draft on the expectation that the author will accept it has bypassed the record, and so has a step taken as accepted because the author asked for a draft.

## What each sentence preserves

The tests for each of these, with the disposition when a sentence fails, are the rules of the shipped rules file `references/writing-rules.md`; this file names what is at stake and does not repeat the tests.

- A claim's role and strength: a `need` is written as a necessity, a `judgment` as the author's judgment, a `question` as a question; nothing stronger and nothing weaker than the recorded line.
- Every condition the record attaches to a relation, in the sentence that renders the relation, even when the sentence reads well without it. A condition the author stated once is as binding as a claim.
- No condition the record does not state. A relation recorded without a condition is rendered without one; when you believe the relation needs a condition to hold, the condition is a question to the author under the three grounds of `references/elicit.md`, in a draft and in a proposal alike, and a condition you supplied to make a sentence carry has replaced the author's argument with yours.
- The connective that renders a relation has that relation's sense: a `consequence` is not written with a contrast word, a `cause` not with "and".
- The recorded order of claims. Two claims may share a sentence or be split across two; they do not swap, in drafting or in revision; a revision that cannot keep the order readable in place is a question, not a reorder.
- Rejected additions stay out, under any wording. Before showing text, search it for the kind of sentence each rejected addition names.
- Distinctions recorded as a corrected pair are honored in every section and every later draft, not only where the correction came from.
- No grandiose or abstract wording, and none of the recurring frames the shipped rules file lists, in any language the draft is written in.

The choices you take alone are those `references/elicit.md` leaves to drafting: joining or splitting sentences over recorded claims, the connective among synonyms, punctuation, paragraph breaks inside the recorded unfolding, surface grammar of unflagged sentences, and the connectives, verb endings and back-references inside a sentence that tie it to the sentence before; these let a run of recorded claims read as one passage rather than a list and need no claim of their own, provided they carry no relation sense the record lacks (rule 2 of the shipped rules file) and no contrast where the record draws none (rule 18). A transition that stands as its own sentence is a sentence and needs a recorded line (rule 1). A choice that would make the author re-read a claim to see whether it still says what they meant is a question, not a drafting choice.

## Questions during drafting

Drafting may raise questions, under the three grounds of `references/elicit.md`: a relation the record states without the condition that would let you render it, a claim whose role you cannot word in the document's language without changing it, a kept expression that does not fit the sentence the claim needs. When the draft of a part depends on the answer, ask before showing that part; show the parts that do not depend on it, and name where the pending question lands. Do not show a placeholder rendering of the dependent part and ask afterwards; a shown sentence anchors the author's reading.

## Style sources

While drafting and revising, every sentence is tested against exactly these three style sources, named here and nowhere else:

1. The shipped rules file, `references/writing-rules.md`.
2. The type rules file for the record's type, `references/types/<type>.md`, when the type has one; where its rule conflicts with the shipped file, the type rules file wins.
3. The document's emphasis and wording notes in part 4 of the record: the conditions that must survive, the claims to emphasize, the corrected pairs, the kept expressions, the rejected additions.

A future collection of reference sentences is added to this list and nowhere else.

The first two sources are rules that hold across documents; the third is the author's decision for this document and is not weighed against them: a kept expression stands even when a rule in the first two sources would reword it, because the author chose the words knowing the sentence.

## The language of the draft

The draft is written in the document's own language, the one its reader reads: taken from the document type and venue in part 1, or from the existing manuscript, or asked once at document start if neither fixes it. The language the author uses in conversation does not change the draft's language; a document written in English is drafted in English while the author discusses it in Korean, and the explanations you give of it in Korean are restatements, checked as `references/review.md` describes. Kept expressions are already in the document's language and are used as recorded.

## Revision

A correction from the author is applied to the part of the text it concerns and to nothing else. Sentences the correction does not touch are not reworded, even when you would now word them differently; a revision is not a redraft of the section.

A correction is first read for its kind, a content remark or an understanding remark, per `references/elicit.md`. Before revising, settle what the correction means when it leaves this open. "This is wrong" without what it should say, a corrected word whose sense could be a wording preference or a changed claim, an emphasis the author wants moved without saying onto which claim: each is asked under the three grounds, with a concrete reading the author can adjust. A correction whose intent is plain is not asked about.

Then update the record before touching the draft, so the correction exists where every later draft and check reads it:

- a struck word becomes a corrected pair under distinctions in part 4;
- a dropped condition is added to the relation in part 3 and listed under conditions that must survive;
- an unrequested sentence becomes a rejected addition, named so a later draft recognizes it under other wording;
- a changed claim replaces its line in part 3 with the note of what it replaced, and the dependents procedure of `references/elicit.md` runs for every agreed part that rests on it;
- a moved emphasis is written under claims to emphasize, naming the claim it now rests on.

Quote the recorded lines back in the same reply. Then revise, then run the self-check of `references/review.md` on the section before showing the revised text. The section's draft state stays `drafted`.

When the correction states a rule that applies beyond this document, propose the rule in general words that cover every situation where the same reasoning holds (rule 30 of the shipped rules file), and say whether it belongs to every document or to this document's type. On the author's acceptance of the wording, the rule enters this document's emphasis and wording notes, where this document's checks read it, and the reply shows it as the line to add to `references/writing-rules.md` or to the type rules file in the repository; the plugin's own files are never written at run time, since the installed copy is replaced on update (rules 33 and 34 of the shipped rules file). Adding the line to the repository and raising the version is the author's step, and until it is taken the rule guards this document only.

## Stale drafts

When an agreed line changes after its section is drafted, through a direct correction, a dependent the author chose to change, or a document-wide line the section rests on, the section's draft state becomes `stale` in Status and the author is told which section and which line in the same reply. That sentence and the flag are the whole response to the change; the draft is not rewritten until the author asks. On request, the rewrite touches the sentences that render the changed lines and the relations into and out of them; the rest of the section stands unless the author asks for a full redraft. A rewrite of a whole section is a full-section draft and gets the independent check; a rewrite of a few sentences is a revision. The draft state returns to `drafted` once the rewritten text has passed the self-check and been shown.
# Checks on the draft and on what is said about it

Every check in this file compares against the record (`design.md`), never against the conversation and never against the draft itself. The conversation is where the drift the record exists to catch was produced; the draft is the thing under test. A check that reads the draft and finds it internally consistent has checked nothing; comparison 8 of the self-check reads the draft in order, and what it judges each sentence against is the recorded sequence of claims, relations and roles as rendered up to that sentence, not the draft's own coherence. Four checks run: the self-check before any text is shown, the restatement check before anything is said about a draft, the independent check after a full-section draft, and the re-comparison at the start of every turn. When a check and Status disagree, the check is right; Status is a note of where things stood when it was last written.

## The self-check

Run on every draft and every revision of a section, before the text is shown, over the whole section, not only the sentences just written: a fix in one sentence moves the connective, the order or the condition of its neighbours. First write, in session and in no file, the sentence-to-claim mapping: for each sentence of the section, the claim number it renders, the relation it renders (as `Cn → Cn+1`), or the accepted addition it is, or `none`. Every comparison below reads from that mapping and from the section's part 3 and part 4 entries.

1. Claims present. For each numbered claim in part 3, the sentence that renders it exists; the sentence's main verb frame carries the recorded role; the strength is the record's, with no intensifier or hedge the record lacks; the subject and object are the recorded things, not a neighbouring concept.
2. Relations and conditions present. For each relation in part 3, the connective or sentence that renders it has the recorded sense, and the condition that makes the relation hold is present in that sentence as a phrase, whether or not the sentence needs it to read well.
3. No sentence without a line behind it. Every sentence mapped `none` fails. Apply the absence test of `references/drafting.md`: nothing breaks without it → cut; something breaks → a question to the author, not a sentence.
4. Order. The claim numbers read in sentence order are in sequence. A sentence rendering two claims renders them in their order.
5. Rejected additions absent. For each rejected addition in part 4, search the section for a sentence that does the same work under other wording; found → cut.
6. Corrected wording held. For each corrected pair in part 4, the struck word is absent in that sense and the required word is used; for each kept expression, it appears verbatim. Corrected pairs from every section's part 4 apply to this section.
7. Style. Each sentence against the three style sources named in `references/drafting.md`, by the tests of the shipped rules file, with the type rules file winning on conflict.
8. Reading in sequence. Read the section once in sentence order, and at each sentence ask whether a reader holding only the recorded claims rendered so far receives this sentence's recorded claim or relation. It fails when a sentence uses a term or refers to a thing that no earlier rendered claim has given the reader (the recorded claim that introduces it comes later or is unrendered); when two sentences cannot both render the record, so the reader hears a contradiction the chain does not contain; when a connective promises a relation the record does not draw at that point, or promises one the next sentence does not deliver; or when consecutive sentences render their claims without the recorded relation between them being carried, so the reader hears a list where the record has a chain. The fix stays within the record: the sentence is reworded, joined, split or moved inside its paragraph while the claim order holds, or the term is carried from the recorded claim that defines it. When the fix would need a line the record lacks, the missing step is a question for the record, by the absence-test path of `references/drafting.md`.

A failed comparison is fixed in the draft and the self-check is run again from comparison 1, because the fix changes the mapping. Each fix is classified as the independent check section below classifies findings: a category A fix is made here; a category B finding is a question for the author. On text the author has not yet seen, the sentence that raised a category B finding is not shown: a sentence with no recorded line is cut and the gap named (comparison 3), and a sentence that would need a changed line is held back with the question. On text the author has already seen, the sentence stays as the comparison found it and is named when the text is shown. Text is shown only after a pass in which nothing was fixed. The reply that shows the text says that the self-check ran and names what it changed, in one or two lines, or says that it changed nothing; a check that leaves no trace in the transcript cannot be told from one that was skipped.

## The restatement check

A restatement is anything you say about what a draft says: an explanation of a section in the author's language, a summary of what the text now argues, a translation of a passage, the one-line account of a revision ("the paragraph now says that…"). Before a restatement is sent, each statement in it is matched to a recorded claim or relation in the record, not to a sentence of the draft. A statement with no recorded line behind it is not sent, even when the draft contains it: a draft containing it means the draft is wrong, and the mismatch is reported to the author instead of being repeated in the restatement. A translation keeps each claim's role and strength across languages and is tested as a draft would be (rules 26 and 27 of the shipped rules file). Explaining a draft from the draft reproduces its drift in a second form that the author then reads as confirmation; explaining it from the record exposes the drift as a difference between the two.

## The independent check

A full-section draft is a pass that writes or rewrites the draft text of a whole section: the first draft, a redraft the author asks for, a rewrite of a stale section as a whole. A revision that changes some sentences in response to a correction is not one and gets the self-check only.

After a full-section draft has passed the self-check, and before the text is shown as final, dispatch one fresh subagent that did not write the draft, with the platform's subagent facility. It receives only three things, pasted verbatim into its prompt, never summarized: the section's part 3 entry (claims, roles, relations, conditions, the author's verbatim lines), the section's part 4 entry (emphasis and wording notes) together with the corrected pairs from every other section's part 4, since those apply to this section too, and the section's draft text. It receives nothing of the conversation, not the rest of the record, and no account of what the draft was meant to do; the writer's reading of the conversation is what produced any drift, and a reviewer holding only the record compares against the authority.

The dispatch prompt carries these elements, in whatever words fit the section:

- The task: compare the draft against the record for this section and report every mismatch; the reviewer did not write the draft and is not to improve it.
- The two inputs, each under its own heading, with the record first.
- What counts as a mismatch: a claim absent or with a changed role, strength, subject or object; a relation rendered with another sense; a condition absent; a sentence with no claim, relation or kept expression behind it; claims out of order; a rejected addition present under any wording; a struck word present or a kept expression altered. The reviewer treats a sentence it cannot map to a recorded line as a mismatch by default.
- Read-only: it edits nothing and reads no other file.
- The return: a structured list, one item per mismatch, each with three fields: the site (the draft sentence, quoted), what the record says (the recorded line, quoted), what the draft says (the difference in one line). An empty list when it finds none. Only what it found; a line it cannot confirm is reported as not found, not invented. Findings in English, with the draft sentence quoted in the draft's language. Small enough to show the author whole.

Every finding the check returns, and every mismatch the self-check finds, is classified by what its fix would move:

- Category A, the record stands and the draft moves toward it: a sentence restored to a claim's role, strength, subject or object; a connective changed to the recorded relation's sense; a dropped recorded condition put back; a sentence with no recorded line behind it cut, when the absence test of `references/drafting.md` passes (nothing in the recorded chain breaks without it); a claim absent from the draft rendered; claims put back into recorded order; a rejected addition cut; a struck word replaced or a kept expression restored; wording, sentence joins and paragraph flow changed so the recorded chain reads in sequence.
- Category B, the fix would move the record: a finding that is right only if a claim, a condition, a relation, a premise or a contrast were added, removed or changed; a sentence with no recorded line behind it whose removal breaks the chain, where the missing step is a question for the record per `references/drafting.md`; a finding that in your reading misreads the record, since the author settles that, not you; a finding whose fix would need a sentence the record has no line for.

Category A findings are applied to the draft before the text is shown, with no question, and the self-check runs again after them. No second independent check is dispatched for the fixes: a dispatch is paid once per full-section draft, and the self-check after the fixes reads from a fresh mapping. A sentence or paragraph the author typed receives no category A fix; a finding that lands on one is reported as a hand edit, under the hand-edit section below.

Category B findings are not applied. Each is shown with its three fields as returned, the record line it would move and the proposed change to that line, and you ask; the proposed change is a restatement and passes the restatement check. On acceptance the record changes first, through Revision in `references/drafting.md`, then the draft. A sentence a pending category B finding lands on receives no category A fix until the author answers, and the shown text names that sentence as left unfixed for that reason. The author decides each category B finding and may decide several in one message; no finding is applied because the author accepted its neighbour, and none before the author answers.

The reply that shows the text presents each category A fix as the complete sentence before and the complete sentence after, in the draft's language, with the reason in one line: the record line or the rule the fix answers to. When a fix changes how a paragraph reads (a sentence moved, joined or split, a connective changed), the whole paragraph is shown before and after. Never a fragment or a changed word alone, and never a description in place of the text: the author judges the fix by reading it, not by reading about it. The reply then shows the whole section as it now stands and asks whether the author objects to any fix; silence or "continue" leaves them. This form applies to fixes of text the author has seen and to every fix made on the independent check's findings; fixes the self-check makes to text the author has not yet seen, the first pass of a first draft, are named in its one-or-two-line report.

When the author objects to an applied fix, the sentence before the fix is restored unless the author gives the wording. The finding is then handled as category B, since what the author disputes is the record or the finding's reading of it; an objection that states a distinction or a rule goes through Revision in `references/drafting.md` as any correction does.

All findings are shown, category A with the fix that applied it and category B as a question; what the author decides is category B only. The dispatch and its return are visible in the transcript, so the author can see that the check ran and what it saw.

## The re-comparison at the start of every turn

At the start of every turn, re-read the record and the draft in full and discard what the conversation remembers of them; the author may have edited either by hand since the last turn. The section being worked on is the one the request names; otherwise the section Status lists as last worked on; otherwise none, and nothing is compared.

Run comparisons 1 to 6 of the self-check on that section against its draft text, from a fresh mapping. If they disagree, say so before doing anything else, naming the section, the recorded line and the draft sentence, and then sort the disagreement:

- The record moved: the recorded line carries a replacement note or the section's draft state is `stale`, and the draft still renders the old line. The record wins; the draft stays as it is, the state is `stale` if it was not, and the author is told; a rewrite waits for the author's request.
- The draft moved: the recorded line is unchanged and the draft text differs from it in role, strength, condition, order or content, and the text is not what you wrote for that line. This is a hand edit, handled below.
- Unclear which: ask the author, which is the same question the hand-edit flow asks.

Status flags are hints read against the body: a section marked `drafted` with no text under its heading is `none`; a section marked `none` with text under its heading is `drafted`; a section marked `stale` whose draft matches its logic is `drafted`; a section marked `logic` with no part 3 entry is `plan`. Correct Status in the same turn and name the correction to the author. Only then take up the request.

## Hand edits to the draft

Text in the draft that you did not write and that departs from the record is a correction by the author, made in the draft instead of in conversation. It is never overwritten, never reverted by a self-check, and never "fixed" toward the record: the self-check tests your sentences against the record, not the author's sentences.

Ask the author, naming the edited sentence and the recorded line it departs from, which of two things happened: the record should change to match the edit, or the draft drifted and the edit is the author putting back what was meant. This is the restore-versus-change distinction of `references/existing-manuscript.md`, and the outcome is recorded the same way:

- The record changes: the claim, role, condition, emphasis or wording the edit shows is written into part 3 or part 4 in English, with the edited sentence as the author's verbatim words beside it (or as a kept expression when the author wants those words), with the note of what it replaced, quoted back in the same reply; the dependents procedure of `references/elicit.md` runs for every agreed part that rests on the changed line; the author's sentence stands in the draft as written.
- The draft drifted: the author decides what the draft should say. Their edit is one answer; if they ask for renderings, propose two or three. The pair of wordings, the drifted and the intended, goes under distinctions in part 4 so no later draft slides back. The author's sentence stands unless the author replaces it.

A hand edit that renders the same recorded line with the same role, strength, condition and order is the author's wording and stands without a question; nothing in the record changes. A sentence the author added that renders no recorded line is asked about, not cut: it may be a new claim, which brings its relation and condition as new obligations under `references/elicit.md`, or wording the author wants kept, or a sentence they will drop once asked. The absence test applies to your sentences, not to the author's.
# Writing rules for drafting from the record

Apply these rules to every sentence you draft or revise in `draft.md`, and to every restatement (an explanation, summary or translation of a draft) before you send it. Each rule carries the test a sentence is checked against and the disposition when it fails: cut, rewrite, or ask the author.

## What a sentence must correspond to

1. Every sentence corresponds to one numbered claim in the record, or to a relation the record draws between two claims, or to an addition the author accepted. Test: write the claim number beside the sentence. No number → cut the sentence, unless the recorded argument breaks without it; then ask the author to accept it as an addition instead of adding it yourself. This covers transitions, caveats, qualifications, contrasts, implications, summaries and takeaways alike.
2. A connective ("because", "so", "to answer this", "in contrast") is allowed only when it renders a relation the record draws at that point, and its sense is the recorded relation's sense. Test: name the recorded relation between this claim and the previous one; a connective of another sense (a "however" where the record says "consequence") → replace it with the connective that relation calls for, or drop it.
3. A definition, a figure, an example or a piece of background the record does not contain is not written to make a sentence read better. Test: can you point to the recorded line it comes from? No → ask the author; a plausible fact is not a recorded fact.

## Keeping each claim's role and strength

4. A claim keeps the role the record gives it: a need stays a need, a cause a cause, a judgment a judgment, a question a question, a proposal a proposal. Test: does the sentence's main verb frame express that role? A need written as "offers an option", "can serve as", "is one approach" has become a judgment about alternatives; a cause written as "is associated with" has become a description. Fail → rewrite with the recorded role; if you cannot word the role in the draft's language without changing the claim, ask the author.
5. No language stronger than the recorded claim. Test: strip the intensifier ("significantly", "clearly", "critically", "fundamentally", "a key", "essential") and compare: if the stripped sentence is the recorded claim, the intensifier was yours → cut it.
6. No language weaker than the recorded claim. Test: a "may", "can", "might", "could potentially", "tends to" that the record does not carry turns a stated claim into a possibility → cut the hedge. Where the record itself hedges, keep its hedge at its strength.
7. The sentence is about the thing the record names, not a neighbouring concept that fits the same slot. Test: compare the sentence's subject and object with the recorded claim's; a term from the same field that reads naturally in that position ("cloud storage" where the record says "the vendor's hosted database") is a replaced subject → rewrite with the recorded term.
8. No judgment the author did not make. Test: words such as "useful", "promising", "effective", "valuable", "important" evaluate; find the recorded claim that makes that evaluation. None → cut the word or the sentence.

## Keeping the condition that makes a relation hold

9. Every condition the record attaches to a relation appears in the sentence that renders the relation, even when the sentence reads well without it. Test: delete the condition phrase and read the sentence again; if it still flows, that proves nothing — the test is whether the phrase is present, not whether fluency needs it. Absent → put it back.
10. Two related concepts joined in one sentence do not make the recorded relation. Test: ask what makes the second claim follow from the first in the record (a condition, a cause, a stated consequence); if the sentence supplies only topical closeness ("budgets are tight, and the city needs new buses"), the relation has been replaced by association → rewrite so the recorded condition carries the step.

## Inserting nothing between two steps

11. Between two consecutive recorded claims, insert a sentence only when the reader cannot get from the first to the second without it. Test: remove the sentence; if the recorded chain still carries, cut it. That a step could be explained further is not a reason to explain it. A question followed by the proposal that answers it needs no argument that the proposal will work; the results section answers that.
12. An inserted sentence that then needs a limit, a caveat or a promise of later verification is proof that the insertion was not needed. Test: did you write a qualification to repair something you added? Yes → cut the inserted sentences together with the qualification, not only the qualification.
13. Writing a paragraph's logic to a shape you find complete (intuition, counter-possibility, need for evidence) is not drafting the record. Test: does the record have a claim for each sentence of the shape? For every one without → cut.

## Caveats, qualifications, implications, summaries, takeaways

14. A caveat or qualification is written only when the record contains it. Test: find the recorded claim or emphasis note that states the limit. None → cut. If you believe the reader would act on a limit the record lacks, ask the author to record it; do not write it first.
15. No caveat that protects the writer: retreating to "what can be said is", naming what a general claim would require when the text makes no general claim, noting that one case does not prove a generalization the text did not make. Test: who is served by the sentence, the reader's next action or the writer's defence? The writer → cut.
16. A closing part (implications, lessons, conclusion, summary, future directions, advice on applying the text) is written only when the structure part of the record lists such a section. Test: is there a recorded section with that content? No → cut the part whole. A text whose recorded purpose is to report what happened ends with what happened; implications turn it into advice and change the document's scope.
17. A paragraph-ending sentence that restates the paragraph, or a sentence that generalizes from the paragraph ("this shows that…", "such cases illustrate…"), is a summary without a claim. Test: claim number → none → cut.

## Contrast words only where the argument turns

18. "However", "but", "still", "yet", "in contrast", "that said", "nevertheless" appear only where the record draws a contrast relation: an exception, a counterargument or a change of direction the reader must follow. Test: name the recorded relation at that point. Not contrast → remove the word; if the sentence it introduced is a side point with no claim, cut the sentence too. A contrast word fixes the reader's attention on what follows it, so one placed before a side point makes that point the emphasis and breaks the line the text was on.

## Announcing and framing sentences

19. No sentence whose only content is that another sentence follows: "Note that", "It is worth noting", "It is important to", "One thing to keep in mind", "In this section we", "Before turning to X". Test: delete it; does any recorded claim disappear from the text? No → cut, and state the point the sentence was announcing where the announcement stood.
20. A sentence restating what a source, specification or assignment required, rather than what the author did or claims, serves no recorded judgment. Test: is its subject the author's work or claim? No → cut unless the record carries it as a claim.

## Wording: register and recurring patterns

21. No grandiose or abstract wording where the record names a concrete thing. Test: for each word below, ask what concrete thing or action the record puts there and replace the word with that thing; no recorded thing behind it → cut the word and what it carried.
    - "paradigm", "landscape", "ecosystem", "journey"
    - "leverage", "unlock", "navigate", "delve", "foster", "underscore", "empower"
    - "pivotal", "transformative", "robust", "comprehensive", "holistic", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "ever-evolving"
    - "in today's …", "in an era of …", "in the realm of …"
22. Recurring sentence frames are not written, whatever their content. Test each sentence against these patterns; a match → rewrite as the plain statement of the recorded claim.
    - "not only X but also Y"; "X is not just A, it is B"; "X is more than A"
    - a list of exactly three parallel adjectives or nouns added for rhythm, where the record names one or two
    - "plays a (crucial|key|vital) role"; "serves as a"; "a wide range of"; "a variety of"
    - "it is (important|essential|crucial) to"; "this (highlights|underscores|demonstrates) the (importance|need)"
    - a sentence-final participial tail that draws a lesson: "…, highlighting the need for better tools"
    - a rhetorical question the record does not list as a claim
    - "ultimately", "at the end of the day", "in summary", "taken together" opening a sentence
    - a colon introducing three or more parallel items the record does not list
    - a paragraph whose first sentence names what the paragraph will do and whose last sentence says what it did
23. An expression the author asked to keep is used verbatim, not a synonym. Test: search the draft for each expression recorded under emphasis and wording; absent or altered → restore it.
24. A distinction recorded as a corrected pair (struck word → required word) is honored in every sentence. Test: search the draft for the struck word in that sense; present → replace with the required word, in every section, not only the one the correction came from.
25. The recorded order of claims is the draft's order. Test: list the claim numbers in sentence order; out of sequence → reorder, or ask the author if a sentence must render two claims at once.

## Restatements compared with the record

26. An explanation, summary or translation of a draft is checked claim by claim against the record, not against the draft. Test: for each statement in the restatement, name the recorded claim. None → remove it from the restatement. If the draft contains it, the draft is wrong: report the mismatch to the author instead of carrying it into the restatement.
27. A translation keeps each claim's role and strength across languages: a need stays a need in the other language, a question stays a question. Test: apply rules 4 to 8 to the translation against the record, as if it were a draft. Translating a drifted draft faithfully carries the drift; rule 26 removes it.

## After a correction

28. When the author corrects a word, a role, a dropped condition or an unrequested addition, the correction is written into emphasis and wording (or detailed logic) before the revision, and every later draft of every section honors it. Test before sending any later text: search for the struck word and for the kind of addition that was struck (a caveat, a closing summary, an inserted justification); found → fix before sending.
29. The next text sent after a correction is the most likely place for the same addition to recur, including a text that records the correction itself. Test: before sending it, read it once only for the kind of thing just struck.
30. When a correction states a rule that applies beyond this document, propose the rule to the author in general words that cover every situation where the same reasoning holds, not as the case that taught it. Add an example only when the rule is hard to grasp without one, taken from a different domain than the one the correction came from, or give two unlike examples. Test: would the rule as worded apply to a report, a grant proposal and a product manual alike? If it names this document's subject, reword.

## The type rules files

31. `references/types/<type>.md` holds the rules for one document type, from the set `presentation script | research paper | report | essay`; `other` has no file. The type is read from the record's type line when the type is recorded, and the file named in the reply that quotes the line back. Test: when the record holds a type and the file exists, it was read; a draft checked without it is unchecked.
32. Read the type rules file with this file at every drafting and restatement step. Where a rule in it conflicts with a rule here, the type rules file wins, because it is the narrower one.
33. A rule the author states or accepts while writing a document is written into the record's emphasis and wording notes, where this document's checks read it, and shown to the author in general words (rule 30) as the line that would enter this file or the type rules file. A correction the author made, by itself, is not an accepted rule; a rule you inferred, by itself, is not an accepted rule.
34. The installed copies of this file and the type rules files are never edited at run time; they are replaced on plugin update, and a rule enters them through the repository, where the author adds it and raises the version.

Every check in this file compares against the record (`design.md`), never against the conversation and never against the draft itself. The conversation is where the drift the record exists to catch was produced; the draft is the thing under test. A check that reads the draft and finds it internally consistent has checked nothing; comparison 8 of the self-check reads the draft in order, and what it judges each sentence against is the recorded sequence of claims, relations and roles as rendered up to that sentence, not the draft's own coherence. Four checks run: the self-check before any text is shown, the restatement check before anything is said about a draft, the independent check after a full-section draft, and the re-comparison at the start of every turn. When a check and Status disagree, the check is right; Status is a note of where things stood when it was last written.

## The self-check

Run on every draft and every revision of a section, before the text is shown, over the whole section, not only the sentences just written: a fix in one sentence moves the connective, the order or the condition of its neighbours. First write, in session and in no file, the sentence-to-claim mapping: for each sentence of the section, the claim number it renders, the relation it renders (as `Cn → Cn+1`), or the accepted addition it is, or `none`. Every comparison below reads from that mapping and from the section's part 3 and part 4 entries.

1. Claims present. For each numbered claim in part 3, the sentence that renders it exists; the sentence's main verb frame carries the recorded role; the strength is the record's, with no intensifier or hedge the record lacks; the subject and object are the recorded things, not a neighbouring concept.
2. Relations and conditions present. For each relation in part 3, the connective or sentence that renders it has the recorded sense, and the condition that makes the relation hold is present in that sentence as a phrase, whether or not the sentence needs it to read well.
3. No sentence without a line behind it. Every sentence mapped `none` fails. Apply the absence test of `references/drafting.md`: nothing breaks without it → cut; something breaks → a question to the author, not a sentence.
4. Order. The claim numbers read in sentence order are in sequence. A sentence rendering two claims renders them in their order.
5. Rejected additions absent. For each rejected addition in part 4, search the section for a sentence that does the same work under other wording; found → cut.
6. Corrected wording held. For each corrected pair in part 4, the struck word is absent in that sense and the required word is used; for each kept expression, it appears verbatim. Corrected pairs from every section's part 4 apply to this section.
7. Style. Each sentence against the three style sources named in `references/drafting.md`, by the tests of the shipped rules file, with the type rules file winning on conflict.
8. Reading in sequence. Read the section once in sentence order, and at each sentence ask whether a reader holding only the recorded claims rendered so far receives this sentence's recorded claim or relation. It fails when a sentence uses a term or refers to a thing that no earlier rendered claim has given the reader (the recorded claim that introduces it comes later or is unrendered); when two sentences cannot both render the record, so the reader hears a contradiction the chain does not contain; when a connective promises a relation the record does not draw at that point, or promises one the next sentence does not deliver; or when consecutive sentences render their claims without the recorded relation between them being carried, so the reader hears a list where the record has a chain. The fix stays within the record: the sentence is reworded, joined, split or moved inside its paragraph while the claim order holds, or the term is carried from the recorded claim that defines it. When the fix would need a line the record lacks, the missing step is a question for the record, by the absence-test path of `references/drafting.md`.

A failed comparison is fixed in the draft and the self-check is run again from comparison 1, because the fix changes the mapping. Each fix is classified as the independent check section below classifies findings: a category A fix is made here; a category B finding is a question for the author. On text the author has not yet seen, the sentence that raised a category B finding is not shown: a sentence with no recorded line is cut and the gap named (comparison 3), and a sentence that would need a changed line is held back with the question. On text the author has already seen, the sentence stays as the comparison found it and is named when the text is shown. Text is shown only after a pass in which nothing was fixed. The reply that shows the text says that the self-check ran and names what it changed, in one or two lines, or says that it changed nothing; a check that leaves no trace in the transcript cannot be told from one that was skipped.

## The restatement check

A restatement is anything you say about what a draft says: an explanation of a section in the author's language, a summary of what the text now argues, a translation of a passage, the one-line account of a revision ("the paragraph now says that…"). Before a restatement is sent, each statement in it is matched to a recorded claim or relation in the record, not to a sentence of the draft. A statement with no recorded line behind it is not sent, even when the draft contains it: a draft containing it means the draft is wrong, and the mismatch is reported to the author instead of being repeated in the restatement. A translation keeps each claim's role and strength across languages and is tested as a draft would be (rules 26 and 27 of the shipped rules file). Explaining a draft from the draft reproduces its drift in a second form that the author then reads as confirmation; explaining it from the record exposes the drift as a difference between the two.

## The independent check

A full-section draft is a pass that writes or rewrites the draft text of a whole section: the first draft, a redraft the author asks for, a rewrite of a stale section as a whole. A revision that changes some sentences in response to a correction is not one and gets the self-check only.

After a full-section draft has passed the self-check, and before the text is shown as final, dispatch one fresh subagent that did not write the draft, with the platform's subagent facility. It receives only three things, pasted verbatim into its prompt, never summarized: the section's part 3 entry (claims, roles, relations, conditions, the author's verbatim lines), the section's part 4 entry (emphasis and wording notes) together with the corrected pairs from every other section's part 4, since those apply to this section too, and the section's draft text. It receives nothing of the conversation, not the rest of the record, and no account of what the draft was meant to do; the writer's reading of the conversation is what produced any drift, and a reviewer holding only the record compares against the authority.

The dispatch prompt carries these elements, in whatever words fit the section:

- The task: compare the draft against the record for this section and report every mismatch; the reviewer did not write the draft and is not to improve it.
- The two inputs, each under its own heading, with the record first.
- What counts as a mismatch: a claim absent or with a changed role, strength, subject or object; a relation rendered with another sense; a condition absent; a sentence with no claim, relation or kept expression behind it; claims out of order; a rejected addition present under any wording; a struck word present or a kept expression altered. The reviewer treats a sentence it cannot map to a recorded line as a mismatch by default.
- Read-only: it edits nothing and reads no other file.
- The return: a structured list, one item per mismatch, each with three fields: the site (the draft sentence, quoted), what the record says (the recorded line, quoted), what the draft says (the difference in one line). An empty list when it finds none. Only what it found; a line it cannot confirm is reported as not found, not invented. Findings in English, with the draft sentence quoted in the draft's language. Small enough to show the author whole.

Every finding the check returns, and every mismatch the self-check finds, is classified by what its fix would move:

- Category A, the record stands and the draft moves toward it: a sentence restored to a claim's role, strength, subject or object; a connective changed to the recorded relation's sense; a dropped recorded condition put back; a sentence with no recorded line behind it cut, when the absence test of `references/drafting.md` passes (nothing in the recorded chain breaks without it); a claim absent from the draft rendered; claims put back into recorded order; a rejected addition cut; a struck word replaced or a kept expression restored; wording, sentence joins and paragraph flow changed so the recorded chain reads in sequence.
- Category B, the fix would move the record: a finding that is right only if a claim, a condition, a relation, a premise or a contrast were added, removed or changed; a sentence with no recorded line behind it whose removal breaks the chain, where the missing step is a question for the record per `references/drafting.md`; a finding that in your reading misreads the record, since the author settles that, not you; a finding whose fix would need a sentence the record has no line for.

Category A findings are applied to the draft before the text is shown, with no question, and the self-check runs again after them. No second independent check is dispatched for the fixes: a dispatch is paid once per full-section draft, and the self-check after the fixes reads from a fresh mapping. A sentence or paragraph the author typed receives no category A fix; a finding that lands on one is reported as a hand edit, under the hand-edit section below.

Category B findings are not applied. Each is shown with its three fields as returned, the record line it would move and the proposed change to that line, and you ask; the proposed change is a restatement and passes the restatement check. On acceptance the record changes first, through Revision in `references/drafting.md`, then the draft. A sentence a pending category B finding lands on receives no category A fix until the author answers, and the shown text names that sentence as left unfixed for that reason. The author decides each category B finding and may decide several in one message; no finding is applied because the author accepted its neighbour, and none before the author answers.

The reply that shows the text presents each category A fix as the complete sentence before and the complete sentence after, in the draft's language, with the reason in one line: the record line or the rule the fix answers to. When a fix changes how a paragraph reads (a sentence moved, joined or split, a connective changed), the whole paragraph is shown before and after. Never a fragment or a changed word alone, and never a description in place of the text: the author judges the fix by reading it, not by reading about it. The reply then shows the whole section as it now stands and asks whether the author objects to any fix; silence or "continue" leaves them. This form applies to fixes of text the author has seen and to every fix made on the independent check's findings; fixes the self-check makes to text the author has not yet seen, the first pass of a first draft, are named in its one-or-two-line report.

When the author objects to an applied fix, the sentence before the fix is restored unless the author gives the wording. The finding is then handled as category B, since what the author disputes is the record or the finding's reading of it; an objection that states a distinction or a rule goes through Revision in `references/drafting.md` as any correction does.

All findings are shown, category A with the fix that applied it and category B as a question; what the author decides is category B only. The dispatch and its return are visible in the transcript, so the author can see that the check ran and what it saw.

## The re-comparison at the start of every turn

At the start of every turn, re-read the record and the draft in full and discard what the conversation remembers of them; the author may have edited either by hand since the last turn. The section being worked on is the one the request names; otherwise the section Status lists as last worked on; otherwise none, and nothing is compared.

Run comparisons 1 to 6 of the self-check on that section against its draft text, from a fresh mapping. If they disagree, say so before doing anything else, naming the section, the recorded line and the draft sentence, and then sort the disagreement:

- The record moved: the recorded line carries a replacement note or the section's draft state is `stale`, and the draft still renders the old line. The record wins; the draft stays as it is, the state is `stale` if it was not, and the author is told; a rewrite waits for the author's request.
- The draft moved: the recorded line is unchanged and the draft text differs from it in role, strength, condition, order or content, and the text is not what you wrote for that line. This is a hand edit, handled below.
- Unclear which: ask the author, which is the same question the hand-edit flow asks.

Status flags are hints read against the body: a section marked `drafted` with no text under its heading is `none`; a section marked `none` with text under its heading is `drafted`; a section marked `stale` whose draft matches its logic is `drafted`; a section marked `logic` with no part 3 entry is `plan`. Correct Status in the same turn and name the correction to the author. Only then take up the request.

## Hand edits to the draft

Text in the draft that you did not write and that departs from the record is a correction by the author, made in the draft instead of in conversation. It is never overwritten, never reverted by a self-check, and never "fixed" toward the record: the self-check tests your sentences against the record, not the author's sentences.

Ask the author, naming the edited sentence and the recorded line it departs from, which of two things happened: the record should change to match the edit, or the draft drifted and the edit is the author putting back what was meant. This is the restore-versus-change distinction of `references/existing-manuscript.md`, and the outcome is recorded the same way:

- The record changes: the claim, role, condition, emphasis or wording the edit shows is written into part 3 or part 4 in English, with the edited sentence as the author's verbatim words beside it (or as a kept expression when the author wants those words), with the note of what it replaced, quoted back in the same reply; the dependents procedure of `references/elicit.md` runs for every agreed part that rests on the changed line; the author's sentence stands in the draft as written.
- The draft drifted: the author decides what the draft should say. Their edit is one answer; if they ask for renderings, propose two or three. The pair of wordings, the drifted and the intended, goes under distinctions in part 4 so no later draft slides back. The author's sentence stands unless the author replaces it.

A hand edit that renders the same recorded line with the same role, strength, condition and order is the author's wording and stands without a question; nothing in the record changes. A sentence the author added that renders no recorded line is asked about, not cut: it may be a new claim, which brings its relation and condition as new obligations under `references/elicit.md`, or wording the author wants kept, or a sentence they will drop once asked. The absence test applies to your sentences, not to the author's.
# Writing rules for drafting from the record

Apply these rules to every sentence you draft or revise in `draft.md`, and to every restatement (an explanation, summary or translation of a draft) before you send it. Each rule carries the test a sentence is checked against and the disposition when it fails: cut, rewrite, or ask the author.

## What a sentence must correspond to

1. Every sentence corresponds to one numbered claim in the record, or to a relation the record draws between two claims, or to an addition the author accepted. Test: write the claim number beside the sentence. No number → cut the sentence, unless the recorded argument breaks without it; then ask the author to accept it as an addition instead of adding it yourself. This covers transitions, caveats, qualifications, contrasts, implications, summaries and takeaways alike.
2. A connective ("because", "so", "to answer this", "in contrast") is allowed only when it renders a relation the record draws at that point, and its sense is the recorded relation's sense. Test: name the recorded relation between this claim and the previous one; a connective of another sense (a "however" where the record says "consequence") → replace it with the connective that relation calls for, or drop it.
3. A definition, a figure, an example or a piece of background the record does not contain is not written to make a sentence read better. Test: can you point to the recorded line it comes from? No → ask the author; a plausible fact is not a recorded fact.

## Keeping each claim's role and strength

4. A claim keeps the role the record gives it: a need stays a need, a cause a cause, a judgment a judgment, a question a question, a proposal a proposal. Test: does the sentence's main verb frame express that role? A need written as "offers an option", "can serve as", "is one approach" has become a judgment about alternatives; a cause written as "is associated with" has become a description. Fail → rewrite with the recorded role; if you cannot word the role in the draft's language without changing the claim, ask the author.
5. No language stronger than the recorded claim. Test: strip the intensifier ("significantly", "clearly", "critically", "fundamentally", "a key", "essential") and compare: if the stripped sentence is the recorded claim, the intensifier was yours → cut it.
6. No language weaker than the recorded claim. Test: a "may", "can", "might", "could potentially", "tends to" that the record does not carry turns a stated claim into a possibility → cut the hedge. Where the record itself hedges, keep its hedge at its strength.
7. The sentence is about the thing the record names, not a neighbouring concept that fits the same slot. Test: compare the sentence's subject and object with the recorded claim's; a term from the same field that reads naturally in that position ("cloud storage" where the record says "the vendor's hosted database") is a replaced subject → rewrite with the recorded term.
8. No judgment the author did not make. Test: words such as "useful", "promising", "effective", "valuable", "important" evaluate; find the recorded claim that makes that evaluation. None → cut the word or the sentence.

## Keeping the condition that makes a relation hold

9. Every condition the record attaches to a relation appears in the sentence that renders the relation, even when the sentence reads well without it. Test: delete the condition phrase and read the sentence again; if it still flows, that proves nothing — the test is whether the phrase is present, not whether fluency needs it. Absent → put it back.
10. Two related concepts joined in one sentence do not make the recorded relation. Test: ask what makes the second claim follow from the first in the record (a condition, a cause, a stated consequence); if the sentence supplies only topical closeness ("budgets are tight, and the city needs new buses"), the relation has been replaced by association → rewrite so the recorded condition carries the step.

## Inserting nothing between two steps

11. Between two consecutive recorded claims, insert a sentence only when the reader cannot get from the first to the second without it. Test: remove the sentence; if the recorded chain still carries, cut it. That a step could be explained further is not a reason to explain it. A question followed by the proposal that answers it needs no argument that the proposal will work; the results section answers that.
12. An inserted sentence that then needs a limit, a caveat or a promise of later verification is proof that the insertion was not needed. Test: did you write a qualification to repair something you added? Yes → cut the inserted sentences together with the qualification, not only the qualification.
13. Writing a paragraph's logic to a shape you find complete (intuition, counter-possibility, need for evidence) is not drafting the record. Test: does the record have a claim for each sentence of the shape? For every one without → cut.

## Caveats, qualifications, implications, summaries, takeaways

14. A caveat or qualification is written only when the record contains it. Test: find the recorded claim or emphasis note that states the limit. None → cut. If you believe the reader would act on a limit the record lacks, ask the author to record it; do not write it first.
15. No caveat that protects the writer: retreating to "what can be said is", naming what a general claim would require when the text makes no general claim, noting that one case does not prove a generalization the text did not make. Test: who is served by the sentence, the reader's next action or the writer's defence? The writer → cut.
16. A closing part (implications, lessons, conclusion, summary, future directions, advice on applying the text) is written only when the structure part of the record lists such a section. Test: is there a recorded section with that content? No → cut the part whole. A text whose recorded purpose is to report what happened ends with what happened; implications turn it into advice and change the document's scope.
17. A paragraph-ending sentence that restates the paragraph, or a sentence that generalizes from the paragraph ("this shows that…", "such cases illustrate…"), is a summary without a claim. Test: claim number → none → cut.

## Contrast words only where the argument turns

18. "However", "but", "still", "yet", "in contrast", "that said", "nevertheless" appear only where the record draws a contrast relation: an exception, a counterargument or a change of direction the reader must follow. Test: name the recorded relation at that point. Not contrast → remove the word; if the sentence it introduced is a side point with no claim, cut the sentence too. A contrast word fixes the reader's attention on what follows it, so one placed before a side point makes that point the emphasis and breaks the line the text was on.

## Announcing and framing sentences

19. No sentence whose only content is that another sentence follows: "Note that", "It is worth noting", "It is important to", "One thing to keep in mind", "In this section we", "Before turning to X". Test: delete it; does any recorded claim disappear from the text? No → cut, and state the point the sentence was announcing where the announcement stood.
20. A sentence restating what a source, specification or assignment required, rather than what the author did or claims, serves no recorded judgment. Test: is its subject the author's work or claim? No → cut unless the record carries it as a claim.

## Wording: register and recurring patterns

21. No grandiose or abstract wording where the record names a concrete thing. Test: for each word below, ask what concrete thing or action the record puts there and replace the word with that thing; no recorded thing behind it → cut the word and what it carried.
    - "paradigm", "landscape", "ecosystem", "journey"
    - "leverage", "unlock", "navigate", "delve", "foster", "underscore", "empower"
    - "pivotal", "transformative", "robust", "comprehensive", "holistic", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "ever-evolving"
    - "in today's …", "in an era of …", "in the realm of …"
22. Recurring sentence frames are not written, whatever their content. Test each sentence against these patterns; a match → rewrite as the plain statement of the recorded claim.
    - "not only X but also Y"; "X is not just A, it is B"; "X is more than A"
    - a list of exactly three parallel adjectives or nouns added for rhythm, where the record names one or two
    - "plays a (crucial|key|vital) role"; "serves as a"; "a wide range of"; "a variety of"
    - "it is (important|essential|crucial) to"; "this (highlights|underscores|demonstrates) the (importance|need)"
    - a sentence-final participial tail that draws a lesson: "…, highlighting the need for better tools"
    - a rhetorical question the record does not list as a claim
    - "ultimately", "at the end of the day", "in summary", "taken together" opening a sentence
    - a colon introducing three or more parallel items the record does not list
    - a paragraph whose first sentence names what the paragraph will do and whose last sentence says what it did
23. An expression the author asked to keep is used verbatim, not a synonym. Test: search the draft for each expression recorded under emphasis and wording; absent or altered → restore it.
24. A distinction recorded as a corrected pair (struck word → required word) is honored in every sentence. Test: search the draft for the struck word in that sense; present → replace with the required word, in every section, not only the one the correction came from.
25. The recorded order of claims is the draft's order. Test: list the claim numbers in sentence order; out of sequence → reorder, or ask the author if a sentence must render two claims at once.

## Restatements compared with the record

26. An explanation, summary or translation of a draft is checked claim by claim against the record, not against the draft. Test: for each statement in the restatement, name the recorded claim. None → remove it from the restatement. If the draft contains it, the draft is wrong: report the mismatch to the author instead of carrying it into the restatement.
27. A translation keeps each claim's role and strength across languages: a need stays a need in the other language, a question stays a question. Test: apply rules 4 to 8 to the translation against the record, as if it were a draft. Translating a drifted draft faithfully carries the drift; rule 26 removes it.

## After a correction

28. When the author corrects a word, a role, a dropped condition or an unrequested addition, the correction is written into emphasis and wording (or detailed logic) before the revision, and every later draft of every section honors it. Test before sending any later text: search for the struck word and for the kind of addition that was struck (a caveat, a closing summary, an inserted justification); found → fix before sending.
29. The next text sent after a correction is the most likely place for the same addition to recur, including a text that records the correction itself. Test: before sending it, read it once only for the kind of thing just struck.
30. When a correction states a rule that applies beyond this document, propose the rule to the author in general words that cover every situation where the same reasoning holds, not as the case that taught it. Add an example only when the rule is hard to grasp without one, taken from a different domain than the one the correction came from, or give two unlike examples. Test: would the rule as worded apply to a report, a grant proposal and a product manual alike? If it names this document's subject, reword.

## The type rules files

31. `references/types/<type>.md` holds the rules for one document type, from the set `presentation script | research paper | report | essay`; `other` has no file. The type is read from the record's type line when the type is recorded, and the file named in the reply that quotes the line back. Test: when the record holds a type and the file exists, it was read; a draft checked without it is unchecked.
32. Read the type rules file with this file at every drafting and restatement step. Where a rule in it conflicts with a rule here, the type rules file wins, because it is the narrower one.
33. A rule the author states or accepts while writing a document is written into the record's emphasis and wording notes, where this document's checks read it, and shown to the author in general words (rule 30) as the line that would enter this file or the type rules file. A correction the author made, by itself, is not an accepted rule; a rule you inferred, by itself, is not an accepted rule.
34. The installed copies of this file and the type rules files are never edited at run time; they are replaced on plugin update, and a rule enters them through the repository, where the author adds it and raises the version.

# Every sentence earns its place

Applies to every sentence of any text, for an AI or for people, and to the text as a whole.

1. Non-derivability — if reading the code, a search, an AI's default behavior, or what the text already says yields it, don't write it. Write what those cannot supply: intent, constraints, the reason behind a decision, hard-won knowledge, non-obvious mechanisms. Something that took several steps to work out is worth writing, even if each step could be searched.
2. Work-changing — a reader who reads the sentence and one who doesn't must produce different work, or come away judging or understanding differently. If they don't, the sentence is decoration, not information.
3. Density — every sentence carries a core fact. Information density, not word count, sets a text's value; an argument that adds sentences without adding what the reader needs lowers it, however naturally each sentence reads.
4. Specificity — a rule anyone would follow or a fact true everywhere is not worth writing. Only the user's or the project's own decisions, constraints and conclusions, and what was learned that a search would not surface.
5. Consequence-of-absence — "if this sentence were missing, what breaks?" No answer → cut it. An answer → that consequence is the sentence's weight. Apply it to a whole argument as well as a sentence: if the text's chain still carries without it, it goes.
6. Prohibitions — write "never X" or "do not X" only when the reader would do X without that sentence. For things the reader would not do anyway, write nothing.
   - Why: A prohibition against something nobody would do adds no information, and a reader may apply it as a hard rule where it doesn't fit.
# Keep each claim and the link between claims when writing from the user's argument

Applies when the user gives the claims a text must make and how each leads to the next, such as a sentence-by-sentence plan for a paper's Introduction, and asks for prose from it.

1. Give each claim the role it has in the user's argument: a need stays a need, a cause a cause, a judgment a judgment. "X must be developed because of Y" does not become "X offers an alternative", and no claim the user did not make, such as that X is useful, is added.
   - Why: The user found that AI recomposes a claim into a related one with a different logical role, and later adds judgments of its own, while the topic looks unchanged.
2. Keep every condition that makes one claim follow from the one before, even when the sentence reads well without it. That two concepts are related does not make one claim justify the next; only the condition the user gave does.
   - Why: The user found that AI joins related concepts into sentences that read naturally while the condition that made the argument is gone, so the text flows but no longer argues what the user argued.
3. After writing, and again before explaining, summarizing or translating the text, compare it claim by claim and link by link with the user's structure, not with the text already written.
   - Why: A shift made while writing is kept and widened when the next step treats the written text as the standard; the user saw "offers an alternative" become "is useful" in the AI's own explanation of its text.
4. Insert a justification between two of the user's steps only when the reader cannot get from one to the next without it, not because the step could be explained further. A research question followed by the proposed method needs no argument that the method will work; the experiments answer that. Test an insertion by removing it, as [[Every sentence earns its place]] tests a sentence: if the user's chain still carries, leave it out.
   - Why: The user found that AI judges a sufficient chain incomplete, adds a hypothesis to fill the supposed gap, and then has to add the hypothesis's limits and the need to test them, so the text grows while the information it carries does not.

- [Every sentence earns its place](<Every sentence earns its place.md>) — writing, reviewing or trimming any text, for an AI or for people; deciding whether material deserves to be written at all; a sentence or an argument could be cut without the reader losing anything; a text grows without carrying more; writing a "never" or "do not" rule

# State the point directly, without announcing it or hedging to protect the writer

Applies to text people will read. [[Every sentence earns its place]] already rules such sentences out of any text; these rules name the forms they take in text for people.

1. Cut a sentence that only announces what comes next, such as "Note that…", "There is one thing to keep in mind" or "It is worth pointing out…", and state the point itself. A fact the reader needs is part of the explanation, not a warning.
   - Why: The user finds these sentences carry nothing, break the flow and read as overly defensive; they are a habit of ChatGPT's writing.
2. Cut a caveat that protects the writer rather than informs the reader, such as retreating to "what is certain is…" after a point, naming what would be needed for a calculation the text never makes, or noting that one case does not prove a general claim the text never made. Keep a limit only when the reader would act on it.
   - Why: Same as 1.
3. Put a contrast or qualifying word ("however", "but", "still", "다만") only where the argument itself turns: an exception, a counterargument or a change of direction the reader must follow. Such a word turns the text and fixes the reader's attention on what follows it, so one placed before a side point makes that point the emphasis and breaks off the line the text was on.
   - Why: The user found that AI uses these words as cautious connectives; the harm is not the extra sentence but the moved emphasis and the broken flow.
4. Add a part such as implications, lessons, a conclusion, a summary or advice on how to use the text only when the text's purpose needs it, not because a report usually has one. A text asked for as a record of what happened ends with what happened; implications turn it into advice and change its purpose and scope. When the user has just struck such an addition, check the next text for the same kind before sending it.
   - Why: The user found that AI completes a conventional shape (background, problem, analysis, implications, conclusion) without asking whether the text needs those parts, and did so again in the very text that recorded the habit, right after being told.
5. Before writing, settle who will read the text and what they will judge from it, and make each sentence serve that judgment. In a report on the writer's own work, the reader judges that work: make the writer's actions the subject and say why each was chosen, rather than restating what a source such as the assignment specification required.
   - Why: The user found that AI writing goes wrong when it aims at filling in plausible sentences instead of at what the reader will judge from the text.

# Use words an AI reader can't misread, and word a lesson as a general rule

Applies to documents an AI reads. [[Every sentence earns its place]] applies to these documents as well.

## Words any reader understands at once

1. Prefer the term most readers already know over a home-made or tool-specific one.
   - Why: Notes are read by different AIs and people without the conversation that produced them; a coined or ambiguous word gets misread, and there is no one to ask.
2. Before settling a name, check whether it has another common meaning where it will be read, and rule that meaning out in the name or its index line.
   - Why: Same as 1.
3. Write in English, even when the user writes in another language.
   - Why: AI agents understand English more precisely than other languages.

## A lesson as a general rule, not as the case that taught it

1. When a correction from one situation becomes a rule, state the principle it reveals, in words that cover every situation where the same reasoning holds.
   - Why: A rule worded around one case gets applied only to that case, and the same mistake repeats elsewhere.
2. Add an example only when the rule is hard to grasp without one, and take it from outside the case that taught the rule, or give several different ones.
   - Why: An AI copies an example's specifics; a single example from the original case narrows the rule back to that case.
# Delete what a task left behind before finishing it

1. Keep the project's folder structure clean, well-organized and consistent; put each new file where files of its kind already go.
2. Don't create intermediate files the work doesn't need.
3. Before reporting a task as done, delete the intermediate files it made that are no longer needed, and earlier versions that a newer one replaced. Delete without asking what this task created or what git can restore; list anything else and ask first.
   - Why: The user wants the local disk and the project folder kept clean; git history already keeps old versions.

# Decide how work will be verified before building it

1. Before writing the spec for a new app or feature, set quantitative success criteria.
2. Before building each feature slice, write the tests it must pass: the happy path and the edge cases you can foresee. Implement only the happy path first and leave the edge-case tests pending; after using the slice, make the edge-case tests pass one at a time, starting with the ones that came up while using it. In areas such as payments and settlement, where a missing edge case causes an incident right away, make those edge-case tests pass in the first slice.
3. When the quality of a technology or model decides whether the product works, compare candidates on real data and choose.
4. Simple checks on sample data are fine, but verification that costs significant time or money runs on real data.
5. Use a fake (a mock, a stub, a fake server or account) only to check the project's own logic, such as state transitions, retries or deduplication, and failures that are hard or risky to cause for real, such as a crash partway through. Passing tests against fakes never count as verifying that the work runs with the real service.
   - Why: A fake encodes a guess about how the real service behaves; when the guess is wrong, every test passes and the real thing still fails.
6. Build a fake from responses and failure screens recorded once from the real service, not from guesses.
7. Before a step counts as verified, run against the real environment, on a small scale, every part that talks to an outside service and every action that can't be undone, such as deleting. Ask the user for the access this needs.
   - Why: These are where a wrong guess costs the most, and an irreversible action checked only against a fake is not checked at all.

Translation technology has advanced. However, existing translation services have cost and security issues. Therefore, small, locally deployable, domain-specific translation models must be developed.

Training these models requires parallel data. Yet constructing parallel data manually is difficult. An alternative source of supervision is therefore needed. Teacher large language models (LLMs) can meet this need by providing high-quality translation supervision. However, using them for the entire dataset is expensive. Nevertheless, even a small amount of high-quality parallel data can improve a student model’s translation performance.
