# Reading the author's material

Notes, an outline, a pasted plan, a bullet list, an earlier draft, a message the author types in a hurry: all of it is material, never the record. Material tells you what the author has thought about; the record tells you what the author has accepted. Nothing moves from the first to the second without the author's word. The record is written fresh from what the author accepts, in English with the author's words beside each line; the material is never polished in place, because a polished input hardens into an authority nobody approved.

## Classify each fragment by the record part it informs

Read the material fragment by fragment (a sentence, a bullet, a heading, a line of a table) and file each under every part of the record it informs:

- Overview: document type, reader and venue, what the reader will judge, central topic, the message the text must leave, the overall progression.
- Structure and section plans: the sections, their order, what each covers, how each unfolds.
- Detailed logic of a section: a claim, its role (need, cause, judgment, question, proposal, description), the relation that carries it to the next claim, and the condition under which the relation holds.
- Emphasis and wording: a condition that must survive in the text, a claim to stress, a distinction between two words, an expression to keep, something the author refuses to say.

A fragment informs more than one part more often than not: "we must build a local model because hosted services leak data" is a claim with its role and relation (logic), a condition that must survive (emphasis and wording), and a hint about the central message (overview). File it under all three. Filing under too many parts costs one extra look; filing under too few hides an obligation that part then shows as unsettled or, worse, as settled by silence.

Keep the author's own words verbatim wherever a fragment carries a claim, a relation, a condition or a distinction. Your English paraphrase sits beside the author's words in the record later; it never replaces them in the material, because the paraphrase is where meaning drifts.

## A mention is not a statement

Mark each filed fragment by its form: a bare mention (a heading, a keyword, "say something about cost") or a statement that carries its relations and conditions ("cost rules out hosted services for a lab of this size, which is why the model must run locally"). A part of the record that only mentions carry is touched, not settled; every obligation of that part remains open and is asked. A clean, well-organized outline is the case where this matters most: every part looks covered because the outline has a line for it, and none of the lines says what the claim is or why it follows.

## Repetition is emphasis

When the material states the same point more than once, in the same or different words, file it once and record that it was repeated, where, and how. The author returning to a point is the author telling you what matters; it is not redundancy to be merged away. Two statements of the same point that differ in strength, role or condition are not repetition; they are a difference.

## Every difference becomes a question

When two fragments disagree (two notes; a note and an existing draft; a note and what the author says now; an outline's order and the logic's order), do not pick one and do not average them. File both, with their source, and raise the difference as a question of the form "source A says X, source B says Y; which holds?" or "I read these as the same claim stated twice; is that right?". The question still needs its three grounds (where it lands, why the record does not settle it, what goes wrong if it stays open); a difference between two sources on a claim, relation, condition or order always supplies the third.

An input that differs from the author's earlier record may be stale or may be the change the author came to make. You cannot tell which; the author can.

## Nothing is settled by reading

Reading material produces, in session and in no file: the fragments filed per record part, each with its form marker and its verbatim words; the repetitions noted; the differences flagged as question candidates. It produces no accepted line. When the author's material is rich and consistent, most of it will enter the record almost unchanged, after the author confirms it part by part; when it is thin, the same reading yields little and the questions do the work. A fragment you skim past here is gone: nothing later re-reads the material, so a condition stated once in passing, a number buried in a note, a word the author put in quotation marks, is filed now or lost.

Do not invent what the material does not contain. A missing relation between two listed claims, a missing condition, a missing role is a question candidate, not something to supply from what would usually be said.
