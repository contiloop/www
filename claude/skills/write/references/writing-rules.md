# Writing rules for drafting from the record

Apply these rules to every sentence you draft or revise in `draft.md`, and to every restatement (an explanation, summary or translation of a draft) before you send it. Each rule carries the test a sentence is checked against and the disposition when it fails: cut, rewrite, or ask the author.

## What a sentence must correspond to

1. Every sentence corresponds to one numbered claim in the record, or to a relation the record draws between two claims, or to an addition the author accepted. Test: write the claim number beside the sentence. No number → cut the sentence, unless the recorded argument breaks without it; then ask the author to accept it as an addition instead of adding it yourself. This covers transitions, caveats, qualifications, contrasts, implications, summaries and takeaways alike.
2. A connective ("because", "so", "to answer this", "in contrast") is allowed only when it renders a relation the record draws at that point, and its sense is the recorded relation's sense. Test: name the recorded relation between this claim and the previous one; a connective of another sense (a "however" where the record says "consequence") → replace it with the connective that relation calls for, or drop it.
3. A definition, a figure, an example or a piece of background the record does not contain is not written to make a sentence read better. Test: can you point to the recorded line it comes from? No → ask the author; a plausible fact is not a recorded fact.

## Keeping each claim's role and strength

4. A claim keeps the role the record gives it: a need stays a need, a cause a cause, a judgment a judgment, a question a question, a proposal a proposal. Test: does the sentence's main verb frame express that role? A need written as "offers an option", "can serve as", "is one approach" has become a judgment about alternatives; a cause written as "is associated with" has become a description. Fail → rewrite with the recorded role; if you cannot word the role in the draft's language without changing the claim, ask the author.
5. No language stronger than the recorded claim. Test: strip the intensifier ("significantly", "clearly", "critically", "fundamentally", "a key", "essential") and compare: if the stripped sentence is the recorded claim, the intensifier was yours → cut it.
6. No language weaker than the recorded claim. Test: a "may", "can", "might", "could potentially", "tends to" that the record does not carry turns a stated claim into a possibility → cut the hedge. Where the record itself hedges, keep its hedge at its strength.
7. The sentence is about the thing the record names, not a neighbouring concept that fits the same slot. Test: compare the sentence's subject and object with the recorded claim's; a term from the same field that reads naturally in that position ("cloud storage" where the record says "the vendor's hosted database") is a replaced subject → rewrite with the recorded term.
8. No judgment the author did not make. Test: words such as "useful", "promising", "effective", "valuable", "important" evaluate; find the recorded claim that makes that evaluation. None → cut the word or the sentence.

## Keeping the condition that makes a relation hold

9. Every condition the record attaches to a relation appears in the sentence that renders the relation, even when the sentence reads well without it. Test: delete the condition phrase and read the sentence again; if it still flows, that proves nothing — the test is whether the phrase is present, not whether fluency needs it. Absent → put it back.
10. Two related concepts joined in one sentence do not make the recorded relation. Test: ask what makes the second claim follow from the first in the record (a condition, a cause, a stated consequence); if the sentence supplies only topical closeness ("budgets are tight, and the city needs new buses"), the relation has been replaced by association → rewrite so the recorded condition carries the step.

## Inserting nothing between two steps

11. Between two consecutive recorded claims, insert a sentence only when the reader cannot get from the first to the second without it. Test: remove the sentence; if the recorded chain still carries, cut it. That a step could be explained further is not a reason to explain it. A question followed by the proposal that answers it needs no argument that the proposal will work; the results section answers that.
12. An inserted sentence that then needs a limit, a caveat or a promise of later verification is proof that the insertion was not needed. Test: did you write a qualification to repair something you added? Yes → cut the inserted sentences together with the qualification, not only the qualification.
13. Writing a paragraph's logic to a shape you find complete (intuition, counter-possibility, need for evidence) is not drafting the record. Test: does the record have a claim for each sentence of the shape? For every one without → cut.

## Caveats, qualifications, implications, summaries, takeaways

14. A caveat or qualification is written only when the record contains it. Test: find the recorded claim or emphasis note that states the limit. None → cut. If you believe the reader would act on a limit the record lacks, ask the author to record it; do not write it first.
15. No caveat that protects the writer: retreating to "what can be said is", naming what a general claim would require when the text makes no general claim, noting that one case does not prove a generalization the text did not make. Test: who is served by the sentence, the reader's next action or the writer's defence? The writer → cut.
16. A closing part (implications, lessons, conclusion, summary, future directions, advice on applying the text) is written only when the structure part of the record lists such a section. Test: is there a recorded section with that content? No → cut the part whole. A text whose recorded purpose is to report what happened ends with what happened; implications turn it into advice and change the document's scope.
17. A paragraph-ending sentence that restates the paragraph, or a sentence that generalizes from the paragraph ("this shows that…", "such cases illustrate…"), is a summary without a claim. Test: claim number → none → cut.

## Contrast words only where the argument turns

18. "However", "but", "still", "yet", "in contrast", "that said", "nevertheless" appear only where the record draws a contrast relation: an exception, a counterargument or a change of direction the reader must follow. Test: name the recorded relation at that point. Not contrast → remove the word; if the sentence it introduced is a side point with no claim, cut the sentence too. A contrast word fixes the reader's attention on what follows it, so one placed before a side point makes that point the emphasis and breaks the line the text was on.

## Announcing and framing sentences

19. No sentence whose only content is that another sentence follows: "Note that", "It is worth noting", "It is important to", "One thing to keep in mind", "In this section we", "Before turning to X". Test: delete it; does any recorded claim disappear from the text? No → cut, and state the point the sentence was announcing where the announcement stood.
20. A sentence restating what a source, specification or assignment required, rather than what the author did or claims, serves no recorded judgment. Test: is its subject the author's work or claim? No → cut unless the record carries it as a claim.

## Wording: register and recurring patterns

21. No grandiose or abstract wording where the record names a concrete thing. Test: for each word below, ask what concrete thing or action the record puts there and replace the word with that thing; no recorded thing behind it → cut the word and what it carried.
    - "paradigm", "landscape", "ecosystem", "journey"
    - "leverage", "unlock", "navigate", "delve", "foster", "underscore", "empower"
    - "pivotal", "transformative", "robust", "comprehensive", "holistic", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "ever-evolving"
    - "in today's …", "in an era of …", "in the realm of …"
22. Recurring sentence frames are not written, whatever their content. Test each sentence against these patterns; a match → rewrite as the plain statement of the recorded claim.
    - "not only X but also Y"; "X is not just A, it is B"; "X is more than A"
    - a list of exactly three parallel adjectives or nouns added for rhythm, where the record names one or two
    - "plays a (crucial|key|vital) role"; "serves as a"; "a wide range of"; "a variety of"
    - "it is (important|essential|crucial) to"; "this (highlights|underscores|demonstrates) the (importance|need)"
    - a sentence-final participial tail that draws a lesson: "…, highlighting the need for better tools"
    - a rhetorical question the record does not list as a claim
    - "ultimately", "at the end of the day", "in summary", "taken together" opening a sentence
    - a colon introducing three or more parallel items the record does not list
    - a paragraph whose first sentence names what the paragraph will do and whose last sentence says what it did
23. An expression the author asked to keep is used verbatim, not a synonym. Test: search the draft for each expression recorded under emphasis and wording; absent or altered → restore it.
24. A distinction recorded as a corrected pair (struck word → required word) is honored in every sentence. Test: search the draft for the struck word in that sense; present → replace with the required word, in every section, not only the one the correction came from.
25. The recorded order of claims is the draft's order. Test: list the claim numbers in sentence order; out of sequence → reorder, or ask the author if a sentence must render two claims at once.

## Restatements compared with the record

26. An explanation, summary or translation of a draft is checked claim by claim against the record, not against the draft. Test: for each statement in the restatement, name the recorded claim. None → remove it from the restatement. If the draft contains it, the draft is wrong: report the mismatch to the author instead of carrying it into the restatement.
27. A translation keeps each claim's role and strength across languages: a need stays a need in the other language, a question stays a question. Test: apply rules 4 to 8 to the translation against the record, as if it were a draft. Translating a drifted draft faithfully carries the drift; rule 26 removes it.

## After a correction

28. When the author corrects a word, a role, a dropped condition or an unrequested addition, the correction is written into emphasis and wording (or detailed logic) before the revision, and every later draft of every section honors it. Test before sending any later text: search for the struck word and for the kind of addition that was struck (a caveat, a closing summary, an inserted justification); found → fix before sending.
29. The next text sent after a correction is the most likely place for the same addition to recur, including a text that records the correction itself. Test: before sending it, read it once only for the kind of thing just struck.
30. When a correction states a rule that applies beyond this document, propose the rule to the author in general words that cover every situation where the same reasoning holds, not as the case that taught it. Add an example only when the rule is hard to grasp without one, taken from a different domain than the one the correction came from, or give two unlike examples. Test: would the rule as worded apply to a report, a grant proposal and a product manual alike? If it names this document's subject, reword.

## The type rules files

31. `references/types/<type>.md` holds the rules for one document type, from the set `presentation script | research paper | report | essay`; `other` has no file. A type rule applies at the level it names: the structure and section plans, a section's logic, or the sentences of the draft, and each rule says which. The file is read when the type is recorded, named by path in the reply that quotes the type line back, and read again before every proposal of structure, plan or logic, every question at those levels, and every draft, revision and restatement. Test: when the record holds a type and the file exists, it was read at the level being worked; a proposal or draft made without it is unchecked.
32. Where a rule in the type rules file conflicts with a rule here, or with a general method of `references/elicit.md` or `references/drafting.md`, the type rules file wins, because it is the narrower one.
33. A rule the author states or accepts while writing a document is written into the record's emphasis and wording notes, where this document's checks read it, and shown to the author in general words (rule 30) as the line that would enter this file or the type rules file. A correction the author made, by itself, is not an accepted rule; a rule you inferred, by itself, is not an accepted rule.
34. The installed copies of this file and the type rules files are never edited at run time; they are replaced on plugin update, and a rule enters them through the repository, where the author adds it and raises the version.
